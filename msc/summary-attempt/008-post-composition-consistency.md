# Composition Consistency (postulate — axiomatic for the meta-requirement; consequences tiered)

This segment introduces a structural meta-requirement: AAT's predictions must be compatible across levels of description. If a system $S$ and any decomposition of $S$ into subsystems $\{S_1, \ldots, S_n\}$ both satisfy the scope condition, then the theory's claims at the $S$-level cannot contradict its claims at the $\{S_i\}$-level. Since the scope condition imposes no restriction on which level the theory applies to, the theory itself must be level-invariant in its predictions. This is presented as an axiomatic structural requirement for AAT's internal coherence — without it, the same observable could receive contradictory answers depending on the modeling boundary.

The postulate has formal teeth. Three composition laws must exist: tempo must be expressible at the composite level as a function of sub-agent tempos plus their coordination structure; the system's persistence must be derivable from the sub-agents' individual persistence plus that coordination; mismatch must compose consistently. The operational consequences are then decomposed into three layers, each with its own home segment in Part III and its own epistemic status: a *scope-selection* layer (which decompositions actually qualify as composites, governed by a teleological-alignment condition), an *admissibility* layer (when composite macro-dynamics are well-posed under conditions (A1)-(A4)), and a *transfer* layer (which Part I/II results lift faithfully to the composite).

The transfer layer carries the deepest result. For **Tier 1** sub-agents — Bayesian updaters on exponential families, linear correctors with positive-definite gain, gradient descent on strongly convex losses — composite contraction admits a closed-form lower bound: parallel composition gives $\lambda_c = \min_i \lambda_i$ (the composite is bounded below by its slowest sub-agent, or equivalently the composite relaxation timescale is bounded *below* by the slowest sub-agent's timescale); cascade composition gives the same up to a coupling-gain adjustment; heterogeneous negative-feedback composition gives a closed-form inequality involving feedback-loop gains and coordination costs. Macro-level persistence then reduces to the same persistence inequality used at the individual level, applied to the composite. For Tier 2 (locally convex, nonlinear) and Tier 3 (non-convex, discontinuous) sub-agents, this transfer holds only locally with degradation, or per-domain, and a qualitative screening test — internal equilibration timescale short relative to external-dynamics timescale — remains useful but not formal.

A striking consequence is highlighted: the standard organizational failure mode (Brooks's Law — adding people to a late project makes it later) is the formal counterpart of the persistence inequality flipping at the composite level. Coordination overhead rises (depressing the composite's correction rate or stretching its equilibration timescale) while external pressure stays fixed (deadline doesn't move), and the same inequality that governs an individual agent's survival governs the team's. Under Tier 1M this mechanism is formal; under looser tiers it is qualitative; whether the coordination-overhead-saturation reading is the empirically dominant cause of Brooks's Law in practice is named as an open empirical question.

A modeling note: a development team is simultaneously an individual developer (an AAT agent), a team (a composite AAT agent), and part of an organization (a sub-agent in a larger composite). The scope condition is satisfied at every level. Composition consistency is what guarantees the theory doesn't return contradictory answers about observables regardless of which boundary an analyst draws.
