# Causal Access — Additional Implications (discussion — chapter-end synthesis)

A chapter-end synthesis that surfaces four threads beyond what the derivation segments themselves emphasize, including one genuinely substantial *negative finding* that does not become statable until both this chapter's machinery and Part I's scope commitment are in hand.

**(1) The loop as Pearl-Level-2 engine — restated structurally.** The framework's distinctive structural break from active-inference and control-as-inference traditions. Those frameworks derive agent behavior from observational learning — Bayesian-network generative models at Pearl Level 1 — and have no native handle on Level 2 outside auxiliary mechanisms. AAT's claim is conservative in form (data is interventional *in character*, not "the agent has cleanly identified causal estimates") but structurally distinctive: the loop's data lives at Level 2, and the framework's results consume that fact. The framework rests on the singular-trajectory commitment from Part I — strip the trajectory commitment and the loop's interventional content collapses. The asymmetry worth carrying forward for ML practitioners: most ML is trained on observational corpora (Level 1) or on offline RL traces with known confounding structure; an agent in deployment, by virtue of being in the loop, generates Level-2 data *for free*. The framework's discovery is that this is load-bearing, not incidental.

**(2) The sandbox hard ceiling — a substantial negative finding.** A consequence of the chapter's central result that does not become statable until the chapter's machinery and the singular-trajectory commitment are both in view. *Sandboxed evaluation breaks the singular-trajectory commitment by design* — sandbox trajectories are forkable (resettable, replayable, parallelizable), and that is what makes sandboxes useful for testing. It is *also* what makes them structurally unable to identify deployment behavior at Pearl Level 2. The sandbox action-observation pair is generated under fork-able execution and is therefore Level-1 (associational) data; the deployment action-observation pair on a singular trajectory is Level-2. By the causal hierarchy theorem, claims about how the agent *would respond to interventions* in deployment are Level-2 questions and cannot in general be answered from sandbox-derived Level-1 evidence, regardless of evaluation thoroughness.

The empirical pattern much-debated in AI-safety literature — "alignment evaluations don't predict deployment behavior" — receives a *structural mechanism*. The problem is not a measurement-quality limitation, not an evaluation-coverage gap, and not a model-capability surprise. It is a Pearl-hierarchy problem, structurally. An entire family of pre-deployment safety-evaluation approaches has a structural ceiling, not a gradient on which more rigor would close the gap. The positive content pairs cleanly: sandboxes are not useless but have a structural ceiling, and *deployment-time monitoring is not substitutable by pre-deployment evaluation regardless of evaluation thoroughness*. The prescriptive consequence — what deployment-time monitoring must structurally look like to discharge what sandbox evaluation cannot — connects to the Information Digital Twin / sidecar monitoring pattern (Hafez et al. 2026; 89% detection accuracy vs 44% for reward-based monitoring).

**(3) One pattern, multiple deployment modes.** The interventional-access result has a generalization that lives in the framework's meta-architecture rather than in any single chapter. Across the identifiability-floor instances, *loop-interventional access provides the unique broadly-available escape from observational-equivalence no-goes* — but the specific interventional quantity varies by layer. **Mode 1 — agent-self-intervention** (this chapter): the agent performs interventions on its own action space as part of the adaptive cycle; the target is the environment's response; this supplies the Level-2 substrate underlying Part II's causal-insufficiency-detection no-go and its single broadly-available escape (joint sibling observability under exploration). **Mode 2 — observer-on-sub-agent** (Part III's contribution): an observer external to a composite performs interventions on one sub-agent's action space; the target is another sub-agent's mismatch-trajectory response; the intervention quantity is the cross-coupling sign that component marginals leave unidentifiable. The unification is at the pattern level (Pearl-$do$ as escape from observational-equivalence no-goes); the specific interventional quantity differs per layer; the framework's posture of *naming a floor, then naming the unique broadly-available escape via its own machinery* is what makes the floors load-bearing rather than discouraging.

**(4) Cost, calibration, and the bridge into strategy dynamics.** The explicit-strategy cost-benefit framing is normative rather than derived, but its *grounding* is in Part I's persistence condition, not an independent design preference. Approaches that consume less tempo budget leave more margin above the persistence threshold; the cost inequality operationalizes that margin into a planning-vs-exploration tradeoff. The cost framing also points at a tractability constraint that bites hard for LLM agents: part of the maintenance cost is keeping the strategy in the agent's representational capacity — for an LLM, fitting the strategy in the context window. A large strategy DAG may exceed this capacity, making simpler strategies (or pure exploration) preferable despite higher exploration costs. This is the same structural fact Part I's bandwidth-as-first-class-prerequisite surfaces in a different vocabulary; the persistence-bandwidth-floor and the strategy-complexity-bound are *the same constraint at different layers*.

The chapter closes by establishing the *substrate*: purposeful action requires Level-2 data; the loop produces Level-2 data on singular trajectories; the data is interventional in character even when clean identification requires more; and the same Pearl-$do$ structure recurs across multiple deployment modes as the meta-architecture develops. Chapter 3's strategy machinery and Chapter 4's strategy dynamics are what AAT does with this substrate.
