# Causal Insufficiency Detection (derived — conditional; exact for shallow cases, robust-qualitative in general)

The framework's first explicit *no-go theorem* on agent self-diagnosis. An agent operating at L0 of the Correlation Hierarchy (assuming its action propositions are causally independent) faces a structural impossibility: under purely on-policy execution, no test, statistic, or Bayesian comparison built from the agent's observable history can distinguish a *latent-cause world* (where siblings share a hidden common cause) from a *no-latent-cause world* whose edge probabilities are matched to the on-policy regime conditionals. The two worlds emit *identical on-policy distributions*. This is the agent-theoretic instance of Bareinboim's Causal Hierarchy Theorem: the L0/L1 distinction is a Level-2 question (concerning what happens under intervention on one sibling) being asked of Level-1 data (on-policy observational distributions).

The construction is explicit for the cleanest case (a two-sibling OR with strict-prerequisite latent common cause): the L0 world's effective edge probabilities are constructed to match the latent-cause world's *conditional* probabilities along the on-policy regime, and direct verification shows the joint distributions over observable events are identical. The no-go is *exact* for shallow strict-prerequisite cases (2-sibling OR or AND with binary common cause) and *robust-qualitative* for general DAG topology, soft facilitators, and deeper structures.

The framework lists the **five escape routes** corresponding to violating each of the no-go's scope conditions. (a) **$\varepsilon$-exploration** breaks pure on-policy execution; gives partial detection scaling with the exploration rate. (b) **Joint sibling observability under exploration** breaks the censoring of short-circuited siblings; gives strong detection via a pairwise sibling covariance test — *the AAT-canonical detector*. (c) **Intermediate-state observability** breaks the censoring at a finer grain; very strong when available. (d) **Structural priors** break the no-prior condition; detection power depends on prior quality. (e) **Direct intervention on the candidate latent** is the strongest route when available.

The **primary detection mechanism** is the pairwise sibling covariance test under intervention. Under the independence model, sibling outcomes under a common parent should be uncorrelated; under causal insufficiency, sibling outcomes are positively correlated. The agent generates test data through routine exploration; on trials where both siblings are observable (the agent tries one and can also observe the other's outcome, or tries them in rapid succession), it accumulates empirical covariance. A statistically significant positive covariance rejects independence and identifies where to add a common-cause node in the strategy DAG. Three preconditions: joint observability (exploration provides uncensored pairs); per-edge credence stabilization (so the empirical means are well-defined); approximate stationarity over the test window.

A **previously-prominent diagnostic** — using the L0 plan-level residual (computed score minus observed success rate) as a detection signal — is shown to be a *degenerate special case* of the no-go: under pure on-policy execution the residual is *identically zero* in both worlds by algebraic identity. The collapse is not a quirk of the residual statistic; it is the no-go's prediction for that specific function. No replacement aggregate statistic can do better. Under partial exploration the residual scales linearly with the exploration rate, with sign matching the dominant node-type bias (+ for OR-heavy strategies, − for AND-heavy), and it becomes a *confirmatory* signal corroborating the covariance test's identification — never a primary detector.

Once detection succeeds, the framework names the construction process to L1: hypothesize the common-cause node, estimate its frequency from the joint outcome data, restructure the DAG by factoring the common cause above the correlated siblings, and re-estimate conditional credences. This is *structural adaptation at the strategy layer* — the agent changes its model class from L0 to L1, adding representational capacity for a pattern the L0 model cannot express, at the standard cost of structural change (temporary performance degradation while credences converge; increased graph complexity).

The framework also distinguishes a new third axis of action — **diagnose** (test known edges for joint correlation structure) — from the standard exploit (pursue current best plan) and explore (test unknown edges for individual success rates). Diagnosis is internal exploration: the agent probes its own model's structural assumptions by *deliberately violating short-circuit* (running both siblings rather than stopping at the first success), generating joint sibling outcomes that on-policy execution forbids.

A subtle but important property: the no-go is *asymmetric*. It forbids on-policy *detection* of L1 from L0 — but it does *not* forbid on-policy *parameter learning within L0*. The agent can learn its L0 conditionals to arbitrary precision on-policy; it just cannot determine whether those conditionals hide a latent. This sharpens the diagnosis-vs-calibration distinction at the strategy layer.

The structural source of the no-go is identified: *sequential short-circuit evaluation* is what makes on-policy data Level-1-only — it censors the joint outcomes that would constitute Level-2 evidence. An agent that did not short-circuit would obtain joint sibling outcomes naturally and the no-go would not apply. But short-circuit is *forced by efficiency*. The no-go is therefore a *tradeoff between execution efficiency and structural diagnosis*; $\varepsilon$-exploration is the AAT compromise — short-circuit by default, occasional non-short-circuit excursions that pay the efficiency cost to maintain detection capability.
