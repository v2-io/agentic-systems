# Credit Assignment Boundary (discussion — discussion-grade, with derived elements)

The strategy-revision loop requires assigning credit for observed outcomes to specific edges in the strategy DAG — AAT's version of reinforcement learning's temporal credit assignment problem. This segment characterizes the *boundary* between tractable and intractable cases, states what the theory requires of any credit-assignment scheme, and identifies what the theory can guarantee *without* solving the problem.

The framework presents a **default signal function** based on gradient-attribution, stated natively in the *log-odds coordinate*. The update is the product of three factors: an outcome residual (what was observed at the plan root minus what was predicted), an attribution component derived from the Jacobian of the plan-confidence with respect to the edge credences (computable in linear time as part of the same forward pass that produces the plan-confidence), and an identifiability coefficient (the regime-modulation from the prior segment, scaling by how cleanly the evidence identifies this edge's causal effect). The log-odds presentation is *forced by the evidential-additivity axiom* (the unique additive-evidence coordinate up to positive affine transformation) and *eliminates a mechanical break* the earlier probability-space presentation suffered (updates outside [0,1]).

The framework names **three things the theory can guarantee without solving credit assignment**. (1) **Persistence is credit-assignment-free**: the sector condition transfers from per-edge credence space to *strategy-plan-confidence error* via the Jacobian — the Jacobian is computable from status propagation; no outcome decomposition required. Persistence of the strategy's plan-level self-assessment does not depend on the agent's ability to attribute outcomes to edges. (2) **The diagnostic framework is plan-level**: the satisfaction gap, control regret, and orient cascade ordering operate on aggregate value, telling the agent *whether* the strategy is failing without requiring per-edge attribution. (3) **Observability-dominance identifies the tractable edges** — only the observable subgraph can receive informative signals at all.

**Three independent intractability barriers** for exact per-edge attribution are named. (1) **Computational**: per-edge contribution has the form of a Shapley value over the AND/OR propagation game, and Shapley value for weighted voting games is #P-complete — exact attribution is computationally hard. (2) **Information-theoretic**: when intermediates are unobservable, per-edge attribution is *underdetermined* — the identifiable subspace has dimension bounded by the number of observable nodes, and when fewer nodes are observable than edges exist, some directions in the parameter space are fundamentally unresolvable from the available data; any attribution in unidentifiable directions relies on priors, not evidence. (3) **The posterior correlation barrier**: even for approximately identifiable cases, any factored representation (independent posteriors per edge) necessarily discards the correlation introduced by failure at multi-parent nodes; the exact posterior complexity grows exponentially with observed failures. The factored representation is an approximation by construction.

The **design requirement** is stated cleanly: any credit-assignment scheme need only satisfy *directional fidelity* — the expected update for each edge must point toward the true credence. This is the per-component version of the gain-sector-bridge condition. Persistence is *robust to approximation*: a sloppy but directionally correct signal function still produces bounded strategic mismatch. The *quality* of the approximation affects the *tightness* of the persistence bound, not whether persistence holds at all.

A useful **hierarchy of credit assignment quality** is presented: Level 0 (plan-level tracking only — persistence guarantee, no per-edge diagnostics); Level 1 (directional fidelity per edge — persistence plus rough per-edge diagnostics, the gradient signal function is the concrete default at this level); Level 2 (proportional blame / expectation propagation — per-edge diagnostics with bias); Level 3 (full Bayesian posterior — optimal but #P-hard in general). AAT's formal guarantees require only Level 0; practical agents need at least Level 1 for adaptive behavior; Level 2 is the sweet spot for most applications; Level 3 is a mathematical ideal computationally unattainable in the general case.

A particularly clean **practical insight** is named: credit assignment is primarily an **observability design problem**, not an algorithm design problem. An agent that *designs* its strategy with observable intermediates (instrumented plans, staged rollouts, checkpoints) sidesteps the intractability entirely. The framework offers the Objectives and Key Results (OKR) framework as a direct organizational instantiation of this principle: the OKR discipline converts a deep, partially-unobservable strategy DAG into one with explicitly observable intermediate nodes (Key Results), making credit assignment between actions and objectives componentwise rather than intractable. Common OKR failure modes — vanity metrics (measurable but irrelevant), too many Key Results (correction capacity diluted), lagging indicators (correction window passes before measurement), Goodhart's Law (metric becomes the goal) — each map to specific AAT predictions about when the strategy machinery would fail.
