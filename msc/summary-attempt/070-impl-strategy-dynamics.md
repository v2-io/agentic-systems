# Strategy Dynamics — Additional Implications (discussion — chapter-end synthesis)

Five threads from the densest chapter in Part II.

**(1) Triple pressure on plasticity in long-lived agents.** The strategy-persistence schema gave a *plasticity lower bound* on forgetting rate — forget fast enough to track non-stationarity. Two complementary pressures complete the picture. **Detection latency**: an appendix derivation shows that without forgetting, the expected cycles to detect a within-class regime change scale linearly with accumulated experience — long-lived agents face detection latency that grows linearly with experience unless forgetting bounds the effective sample size. The framework calls this **stability-induced myopia**: successful agents that have accumulated experience on their working plans systematically take longer to notice regime changes than the same agents earlier in their lives. **Consolidation cadence** supplies a *stability upper bound*: forget slow enough that consolidation can integrate cross-episode patterns before they are discarded. Between the lower and upper bounds lies the **feasibility window**; an empty window is the *catastrophic-forgetting regime*. The three pressures compose: which intervention works depends on which bound is binding. A *short-attention* agent (lower-bound binding) needs retention; a *calcified* agent (latency binding inside the window) needs targeted intervention or external interventional exploration; a *fragmented* agent (upper-bound binding) needs cadence engineering. The same agent in different operating regimes faces different binding pressures.

**(2) On-policy detection structurally fails — identifiability-floor Instance 1.** The causal-insufficiency-detection no-go is the framework's *first formal instance* of the identifiability-floor pattern: an agent at L0 cannot distinguish a world with a latent common cause from a no-latent-cause world matched in on-policy regime conditionals; the two worlds emit identical on-policy distributions. The constructive use of the no-go is what makes it load-bearing: five boundary routes characterize what scope condition must be violated to escape it, and *joint sibling observability under exploration is the unique broadly-available violation* — every AAT agent within agency scope has it via the loop-Level-2 result. The chapter therefore lifts the loop-Level-2 result from "useful machinery" to *the unique broadly-available violation of the no-go*. This is the move that makes the identifiability-floor pattern *load-bearing rather than discouraging* — each floor names a specific machinery's load-bearing role with maximum sharpness. Together with Chapter 3's Cramér-Rao L1' floor (Instance 2), the two cover both *agent-internal* and *self-diagnostic* failures of identification with the same shared escape mechanism.

**(3) Observability dominance and the triple depth penalty.** Unobservable strategy edges freeze; paths through unobservable nodes become epistemically dead absorbing states. This is the structural prediction that organizational departments with poor measurement (R&D, strategy groups, some management functions) develop persistent untested beliefs about their own effectiveness — *structurally* (frozen gain), not motivationally. The same mechanism predicts LLM agents with high-uncertainty intermediate outputs cannot update strategy through those nodes regardless of nominal confidence. The observability result compounds with the chapter's other depth-related results into a **triple penalty on planning horizons**: chain-confidence decay (probabilities multiply, exponential in depth), evidence starvation (the deepest edge faces exponential rate attenuation regardless of upstream reliability), and IB complexity cost (maintaining the strategy has an information-theoretic cost compounding with depth, eventually exceeding the agent's cognitive budget). Deep plans are structurally fragile along three independent axes. The structural pressure toward shallow, parallelizable strategies is grounded in three independent mechanisms, not one. For LLM-based agents working through context-window-bounded planning, the triple penalty is the framework's account of why deep chains of LLM reasoning tend to fail in ways that look "creative" but are actually structural.

**(4) Sub-scope refinements of the persistence template explain optimizer and inference-method folklore.** Two appendix derivations refine Part I's sector-persistence template into sub-scope partitions that explain decades of empirical practice on principled grounds. The **adaptive-gain dynamics** derivation splits the persistence template into sub-scopes: *$\alpha_1$* (fixed-gain Kalman / exponential family / strongly-convex gradient — where the sector parameter is derived from the update rule's structure under directional fidelity); *$\alpha_2$* (adaptive-gain under named meta-gain conditions — where two-timescale Lyapunov composition applies); *$\beta$* (PID / rule-based / human-judgment — where the sector condition is *assumed* per-instance rather than derived). The partition is operationally consequential: *AMSGrad is a meta-gain repair that restores the meta-gain conditions by construction*, which is why it outperforms Adam on ill-conditioned problems — the empirical superiority is now derived, not folklore. *MAML's outer loop sits in $\beta$*, which is why its non-convexity is structurally forced rather than a specific algorithmic accident. The **variational sector condition** extends the persistence template to variational-inference agents under a controlled-KL budget: *mean-field VI suffers a $O(\sqrt{\varepsilon})$ degradation* on the sector parameter because its geometry does not match the Fisher metric; *natural-gradient VI recovers the full sector parameter* because it does. **Mean-field VI agents are structurally suboptimal as adaptive systems by a factor proportional to the square root of the KL-budget, regardless of compute or training data.** The long-running Bayesian-deep-learning debate about MF-VI versus natural-gradient on heuristic grounds has a principled answer.

**(5) Bretagnolle-Huber regret bound and the unified RL convergence theory.** Under deterministic optimal policy — AAT's canonical scope — an appendix derivation shows the Bretagnolle-Huber identity holds *exactly* (not just as inequality), and the strategy-cost regret obeys a tight two-sided bound. The reverse-KL direction in the strategy-cost objective is *forced* by the regret-bound derivation: forward-KL is vacuous under deterministic optimum. This is the *second instance* of the additive-coordinate-forcing meta-pattern: reverse-KL at the divergence layer (after log-probability at the chain layer; with log-odds at the update layer and Fisher metric at the metric layer rounding out the four-layer architecture).

The widest payoff of the chapter is what becomes the **unified RL convergence theory under non-stationarity** once four findings are composed: the two-gap diagnostic from Chapter 3 (local separation between "you're not doing it well enough" and "the world doesn't permit it"); the Bretagnolle-Huber identity here (tight two-sided regret bound); the strategic-tempo and sector-persistence schema (the rate of strategy revision that must outpace strategic disturbance, verified across four canonical topologies); the loop-Level-2 result from Chapter 2 (makes the regret bound *learnable* — interventional data is automatic). The four together give a quantitative RL convergence theory under non-stationarity with three properties no existing RL framework has all of (stationarity, explicit metric structure on policy space, connection to a diagnostic preventing dark-room collapse). The composition is visible only at the chapter level.
