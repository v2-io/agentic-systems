# Satisfaction Gap (definition — exact)

The first of the two orthogonal diagnostic quantities. The framework first defines **objective attainability** as the best achievable value given the agent's current beliefs, available policy class, and planning horizon — the supremum of the value object over policies. The **satisfaction gap** is then the distance between the objective's satisfaction threshold and this attainability: it is positive when the objective is *unmet* under the best available policy given the current model and horizon; it is non-positive when the objective is attainable in principle.

The framework is careful that a positive satisfaction gap does not automatically mean the goal is wrong. The gap has multiple possible *causes*, each with a different remedy: the goal may be genuinely infeasible (revise the objective — but only as last resort); the policy class may be too narrow (structurally expand the strategy); the horizon may be too short (extend planning); the model may be wrong about feasibility (improve the model to reduce epistemic mismatch); or in compound objectives, individual terminals may be satisfiable but their conjunction infeasible. **Objective revision is the last resort, not the first response to unmet goals.** The orient cascade later in the chapter formalizes this ordering.

The framework explicitly notes that the gap value is *convention-relative as a diagnostic*. Under the C1 (one-step improvement) canonical convention, the gap is a *local* diagnostic — it answers "can I improve toward the goal from here?", not "is the goal globally feasible?" A multi-step recoverable objective may show a positive gap because continuation is frozen at the current policy. Under C2 (receding-horizon) and C3 (Bellman), the same gap progressively reveals deeper diagnosis: C2 catches multi-step recoverable cases; C3 means no policy in the class can achieve the objective. The monotonicity result from the value-object segment translates: the Bellman gap is smallest, the one-step gap is largest, and analyses using different conventions cannot be directly compared.

A deliberate **divergence from active inference** is named. Active inference's expected free energy decomposes into pragmatic and epistemic value (how preferred are expected outcomes? how much uncertainty reduction?). The decomposition supports policy ranking but does *not* separate the two distinct diagnoses AAT's apparatus separates: "the goal is unattainable from here" (the satisfaction gap) versus "the current policy is not the best available" (the control regret defined next). Both increase expected free energy without distinguishing the cause. The diagnostic structure depends on AAT treating the objective as a *value functional* on trajectories and attainability as a *supremum*, not on outcomes encoded as log-priors — the active-inference "preferences-as-priors" form ($C(o) = \log P_{\text{pref}}(o)$) collapses the diagnostic by making "wanting $o$" and "expecting $o$" formally the same operation. The framework cites this as the formal response to the *dark-room critique* (Sun & Firestone 2020) of preferences-as-priors formulations.
