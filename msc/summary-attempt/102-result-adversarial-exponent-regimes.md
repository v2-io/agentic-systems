# Adversarial Exponent Regimes (result/observation — conditional)

A refinement of the adversarial-tempo-advantage result. The exponent in the steady-state mismatch ratio scaling is *not a single number*. It depends on two structural features of the disturbance: whether the adversarial coupling enters as deterministic drift or stochastic noise; and whether the coupling dominates the base disturbance rate.

Three regimes. **Regime 1 — Model D (deterministic drift), coupling-dominant**: exponent equals 2 (verified by simulation at 1.999). The framework's headline squared scaling. Derived from the deterministic steady state. **Regime 2 — Model S (stochastic noise), coupling-dominant**: exponent equals 3/2 (simulation: 1.481). The stochastic-disturbance variant where the persistence template's square-root-rather-than-inverse scaling removes one half-power. **Regime 3 — non-coupling-dominant**: when base disturbance is comparable to or exceeds the adversarial coupling, the exponent degrades smoothly to approximately 1 (Model D) or 0.5 (Model S).

The result is the formal statement that the framework's adversarial machinery has *regime-dependent strength*. The squared scaling that makes adversarial dynamics qualitatively different from cooperative dynamics applies only in the coupling-dominant regime under deterministic drift. The exponent decays smoothly through intermediate values as base disturbance grows; in the non-coupling-dominant regime the scaling is roughly linear, and *no qualitative regime change* separates the attacker from the defender.

This has a striking *defensive design implication*. **Cheap noise injection into the defender's observation channel actually helps the defender against a high-tempo attacker** by pushing the regime away from coupling-dominant deterministic drift toward the noise-dominant or base-disturbance-dominant regimes, where the exponent collapses to linear. The structural mechanism is the squared-versus-linear gap: an attacker counting on superlinear scaling to break through the defender's reserve loses the multiplicative advantage when the regime shifts. This is the formal reason highly-noisy environments produce more cooperative-game-like dynamics than expected — high observation noise *gates* the adversarial tempo advantage to near-linear, where the attacker cannot exploit superlinear scaling to drive the defender past the destabilization threshold.
