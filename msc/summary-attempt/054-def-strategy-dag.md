# Strategy DAG (definition — conditional)

The strategy is committed to a formal structure: a directed acyclic graph with **propositional nodes** (each representing a condition that could be true or false, including action-success propositions at the leaves), **directed causal edges**, **causal credences** (one number per edge — the agent's confidence that completing the parent step advances the child step), and **combination rules** at each node (AND or OR). Edges carry a single number; nodes carry one bit (combination type); leaves carry a base credence that updates whenever the model updates. Status propagation through the DAG is a $O(|V|+|E|)$ forward pass that produces a strategy-plan-confidence score at the root.

A subtle structural point: the framework is honest about what is *derived* and what is *chosen*. **Acyclicity is derived** — each node represents a future event at a temporal position, edges require parent-precedes-child, and a cycle would require something to precede itself; the formal step is that a finite set with a strict partial order over time is representable as a DAG. The apparent cycles of iteration (try A, if fail try B, if fail try A again) are acyclic when time-indexed — each attempt is a distinct node at a distinct time. The **Markov factorization** is derived under causal sufficiency via the Causal Markov Condition theorem (Spirtes-Glymour-Scheines, Pearl). What is *chosen* is the parameterization within this strongly-motivated graphical structure: AND/OR combination with single-parameter edges, motivated by parsimony and convergence across three independent formalism attempts.

A *well-formedness* constraint connects the DAG to the objective: the strategy is well-formed when the agent believes that achieving the strategy's terminal conditions yields a trajectory satisfying the objective (with high probability). The terminal conditions are the strategy's *internal operational encoding* of what the objective requires; the objective itself lives outside the strategy. When the agent achieves its terminals but the actual trajectory fails to satisfy the objective, the well-formedness belief was wrong — the operational success criteria didn't capture what the objective required — triggering terminal reassessment.

The framework also presents the **Correlation Hierarchy** as a first-class part of the formalism. The AND/OR propagation computes correct probabilities *if and only if* the strategy DAG is *causally sufficient* — all common causes of any two strategy nodes themselves represented as nodes. When causal sufficiency fails (the dominant case in complex, multi-stakeholder, or adversarial environments — shared infrastructure, common-mode risks, correlated adversary actions), edge outcomes are correlated and the independence model is biased. The framework states the *direction and magnitude* of the bias precisely: with positive correlation from a latent common cause, AND-nodes *underestimate* (clustering of successes helps joint success — conservative bias) and OR-nodes *overestimate* (clustering of failures undermines the redundancy that OR-structure provides — optimistic bias); the magnitude is exactly the covariance, same magnitude opposite sign. Strategies with OR-structure near the root (the typical case — multiple alternative paths to the objective) have net *overestimation* — the agent believes it has more redundancy than it actually does.

Four levels are named. **L0 (Independence)**: tractable baseline — AND/OR propagation as-is, correct when the DAG is causally sufficient. **L1 (Augmented DAG)**: practical sweet spot for *strict-prerequisite* common causes — add common-cause nodes explicitly and factor them *above* the correlation they create as AND-prerequisites. **L1' (Mixture form)**: extension to *soft facilitators* (favorable conditions that help but aren't strictly required) — split the sub-plan into two conditional structures weighted by the common cause's state. Importantly, L1' is *identifiability-obstructed when the common cause is unobservable* (the Fisher matrix is rank-1, the per-conditional decomposition is not estimable); the agent must either augment observability, run multi-child joint observations, or fall back to L0-on-marginals. **L2 (Full correlation)**: arbitrary joint failure distribution — exponential in the number of edges; useful only as a reference point.

A sobering corollary the chapter intro flagged is sharpened here: under purely on-policy execution, an agent at L0 *cannot detect* its own causal insufficiency from its own data — the worlds with and without a latent common cause are observationally indistinguishable. The unique broadly-available escape is **joint sibling observability under exploration**: the agent's interventions through the loop generate the joint outcome data the test requires (positive covariance among observed siblings rejects independence and identifies where to add L1 nodes).

A clarification of the strategy-DAG's relationship to **Moore-machine** representations is offered: they capture *different* aspects of strategy. The Moore machine is the *behavioral surface* (reactive policy: state + observation → action + transition); the strategy DAG is the *epistemic interior* (causal theory of why this is the right thing to do). Neither is strictly more general. Moore machines can represent reactive strategies with no causal model (tit-for-tat, grim trigger) that have no natural DAG form. Strategy DAGs can represent plans with rich conditional structure and uncertainty that would require exponentially many Moore-machine states. The framework uses the DAG because the defining feature of an adaptive agent is that it *revises* its strategy when the model changes — strategy revision requires knowing *why* the agent is doing something, not just *what*. A Moore machine has no internal handle to revise from when the model changes; the strategy DAG carries the causal semantics that revision operates on.

A composition-flavored result is also developed: composing heterogeneous strategy DAGs is an *order-theoretic* condition rather than a magnitude in team size. Exact composition exists if and only if the per-agent partial orders on shared nodes are jointly acyclic and their AND/OR root semantics agree. When shared sub-orders conflict (agent 1 plans A-before-B; agent 2 plans B-before-A), no exact single-DAG macro-strategy exists. The minimal admissible composite is the *condensation* by strongly-connected components — each cyclically-entangled set of shared nodes collapses to one coarse macro-node the composite cannot temporally distinguish, with the irreducible composition defect being exactly the total correlation among the collapsed statuses (bounded by the shared-plan size and crucially *independent of the number of agents*). The composition defect from strategy-topology heterogeneity therefore *cannot by itself flip the composite persistence inequality at large team sizes* — strategy-topology heterogeneity alone cannot cause a Brooks's-Law collapse; only coordination overhead can.
