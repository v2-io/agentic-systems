# Mismatch Dynamics (hypothesis — heuristic; linear first-order ODE)

Mismatch is given dynamics. The linear first-order ODE proposes that the magnitude of mismatch decays at rate proportional to tempo times mismatch (the correction term) and grows at the rate of new mismatch injection from environmental change (the disturbance term $\rho$). The framework is explicit that this is a heuristic — a first-order linear approximation — and the general nonlinear case is what the sector-condition framework handles in the next chapter.

Two steady-state forms appear and are individually load-bearing. Under **Model D** (deterministic bounded disturbance), the steady-state mismatch is $\rho / \mathcal{T}$ — the ratio of how fast the environment changes to how fast the agent adapts. This is the framework's headline inequality in its simplest form, the one that the introduction promised would govern Kalman filters, software teams, and organizations alike. Under **Model S** (stochastic zero-mean disturbance, where the dynamics become an Itô SDE), the steady-state root-mean-square mismatch scales as $\sigma_w / \sqrt{2\mathcal{T}}$ — the square root of the disturbance-to-correction ratio, not the ratio itself. This is one of the volume's more interesting consequences: **correction is less effective against stochastic noise than against deterministic drift**. The $1/\sqrt{\mathcal{T}}$ scaling instead of $1/\mathcal{T}$ is what makes Model S genuinely different from Model D — not merely a notation change but a different physics. The transient solution from initial conditions is an exponential decay toward the steady state at rate $\mathcal{T}$.

The framework is honest about the ODE's status as a fluid-limit approximation of the underlying discrete event-driven dynamics. Under Model D the discrete and continuous steady states are identical; under Model S the variance gap is small when (gain × correction-Lipschitz-constant) is small, with bounded transient error under Lipschitz regularity. The least-accurate phase is initial transients with large gain, but those phases are short-lived.

The segment also previews a result that becomes load-bearing in Part III. When agents are *coupled* — when one agent's actions inject disturbance into another agent — the disturbance term becomes the sum of base disturbance plus *coupling effectiveness × adversary tempo*. Under Model D the steady-state mismatch ratio scales as the **square** of the tempo ratio between the agents (a superlinear advantage following from the $1/\mathcal{T}$ scaling). Under Model S it scales as the **3/2 power** of the tempo ratio (from the $1/\sqrt{\mathcal{T}}$ scaling). These two scaling exponents are the formal core of "getting inside the opponent's loop" being a categorical advantage rather than merely a marginal one.

The segment also names the qualitative shape of the nonlinearities the linear ODE smooths over: saturation at large mismatch (correction mechanism overwhelmed); threshold dead zones (small mismatches go uncorrected); and structural breakdown beyond a critical mismatch where correction collapses entirely because the model class is no longer adequate. These are precisely what the sector-condition machinery in Chapter 4 will handle.
