# Team Persistence (derived — conditional)

The persistence condition lifted to sub-agents within a composite. The construction has two pieces. **Distributed tempo**: an agent's effective tempo now sums direct-observation tempo *plus* communication tempo from allies. Each ally communication arriving at rate times communication gain (from the prior chapter) contributes additively to the agent's correction capacity. The framework notes the channel-independence caveat — when allies report correlated information, the communication tempo *overcounts*, and the additive formula is an upper bound. **Cooperative-adversarial disturbance decomposition**: the disturbance rate an agent faces decomposes into environment, adversarial, and cooperative components, with the cooperative term entering with a *negative sign* — allies acting in the shared environment *reduce* the disturbance the agent faces.

Sub-agent persistence then requires the agent's correction rate exceed the *effective* disturbance rate (environment plus adversarial inputs minus cooperative inputs) divided by the critical-mismatch scale. This is the single-agent persistence inequality with a structured effective-disturbance decomposition substituted in.

The framework names the two cooperative mechanisms — *communication* and *action* — as physically distinct and enters them at *different points* in the persistence inequality. The framework is careful that a given cooperative interaction contributes through *one* mechanism or the other, *not both*. Counting a single ally-event in both would double-count its benefit. The consultant-vs-employee analogy from the chapter intro is sharpened here: communication boosts tempo (the agent corrects faster); cooperative action lowers disturbance (the agent has less to correct). The two are structurally non-interchangeable.

A consequence the framework names explicitly: **teams can persist where individuals cannot**. An individual agent with low correction rate may fail the persistence inequality alone; the same agent operating in a cooperative team — receiving communication and/or having a peer absorb part of the environmental disturbance — can satisfy the inequality. This is the structural answer to why agents form teams: not because teams are intrinsically valuable, but because *they shift the persistence inequality in favor of each member*.

The framework also names the dynamic correctly: cooperative coupling enters as a *negative* term in effective disturbance, so it directly buys persistence margin. Adversarial coupling enters as a *positive* term, depleting the same margin. The signed structure is what unifies cooperative and adversarial dynamics under a single inequality.
