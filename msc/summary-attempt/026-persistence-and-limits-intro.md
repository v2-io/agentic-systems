# Chapter Introduction: Persistence and Structural Limits (discussion — framing prose)

The cycle has been set in motion in Chapter 3; the question now is whether it can keep up. This chapter develops the central inequality of AAT — the persistence condition — together with what happens at and beyond its threshold: where the inequality comes from, when its machinery can be expected to satisfy it, and what fails when it can't.

The first move is honesty about the linear ODE. Real correction mechanisms saturate at large mismatch, threshold at small mismatch, and break down entirely when the agent's model class is wrong for the situation. The chapter replaces "linear correction" with the **sector condition** — that the correction function points inward with at least baseline efficiency $\alpha$ within an operating region of radius $R$. The sector condition captures the qualitative essence of correction without committing to a specific functional form: saturating, sigmoid, threshold, and PID corrections all live inside one Lyapunov argument. Under that condition, the persistence inequality takes a clean form — the agent persists when its baseline correction efficiency exceeds the disturbance rate divided by the operating reserve. Below the threshold, mismatch grows without effective bound up to the sector region's edge. Persistence is not a degree; it is a regime.

The intro flags three pieces of theoretical hygiene the chapter cares about. **First**, the persistence inequality has a thermodynamic shadow: under stochastic disturbance, maintaining the sector-persistence bound requires sustained Shannon information acquisition at rate at least $n\alpha/2$ nats per unit time, with the Kalman-Bucy filter saturating this bound. Survival in this framework is not a state achieved once — it is a *sustained burn rate of information*. An agent cut off from informative observation begins to drift the moment the channel closes; two agents with identical persistence guarantees can face wildly different sustained demands. The threshold says whether persistence is *possible*; the information-rate corollary says what it *costs*.

**Second**, the chapter draws a load-bearing distinction between *structural persistence* (the correction machinery works) and *task adequacy* (the steady-state mismatch is small enough for the agent's actions to remain useful). The two conditions have different remedies and conflating them produces category errors when transferring AAT across domains. A Kalman filter on a moving target satisfies structural persistence whenever it's stable, but task adequacy depends on what counts as "tracking" for the application. A software team can be structurally persistent but task-inadequate. AAT derives the structural condition; task adequacy is a domain parameter.

**Third**, the chapter demotes the sector-condition parameter $\alpha$ from an opaque structural postulate to a derived quantity. The *gain-sector bridge* shows that for agents with directional fidelity in their update rule — including all optimal Bayesian updaters and all gradient agents on (locally) strongly convex losses — $\alpha$ equals the product of the gain principle from Chapter 3 and a worst-case directional-fidelity constant. The sector condition isn't a soft global postulate at all; for the cases the framework cares about most, it is *a property of the gain*. Five named failure modes can break the bridge (directional infidelity, gain collapse, nonlinear saturation, unobservable directions, model misspecification), and each connects to a concrete mechanism rather than being left as residual error.

The chapter then proves two integrative results. *Structural adaptation necessity*: when model-class fitness is too low, no parametric update can close the mismatch floor — the agent must change *classes*, not parameters (the consequence seeded in Chapter 2, now grown to a derived result). *Temporal nesting*: adaptive processes stratify by timescale, with each level operating on the quasi-steady-state output of the level below; the convergence constraint between adjacent timescales means a slower process acting on transients of a faster one produces oscillation. Standard singular-perturbation reasoning, applied here to multi-level adaptation.

A scope coda closes Part I: AAT applies to agents on *singular causal trajectories*. The chronica is not forkable; duplicating the model state and exposing copies to different futures produces two agents on divergent trajectories, neither a sufficient statistic for the other's path. Identity in AAT lives in the trajectory, not in the state — the bridge to the language-constituted and morally-weighted agents that the companion volumes will treat.
