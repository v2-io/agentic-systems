# Mismatch Decomposition (result — exact under fresh-noise assumption)

The first named *result* of the volume. The expected squared mismatch decomposes cleanly into two additive parts: a **reducible model-error term** (the difference between the model's predictive mean and the true conditional mean of the observation) and an **irreducible observation-noise term** (the conditional variance of the observation channel itself, conditioned on environment state and action). The decomposition is the bias-variance decomposition applied to the prediction problem; its cross-term vanishes under the fresh-noise global assumption (observation noise conditionally independent of the past chronica given current environment state and action), which is the standard assumption in state-space models.

The conceptual stakes of this seemingly mechanical decomposition are large. It establishes that prediction error is *structurally persistent* in any realistic adaptive regime — there is a floor below which mismatch cannot be driven by any amount of better modeling, because that floor is set by the observation channel itself. Deterministic, noiseless, perfectly-specified systems are limiting edge cases, not the typical adaptive regime. The total expected squared mismatch is therefore strictly positive whenever either observation noise is non-degenerate or the model's predictive mean is misspecified — and both typically hold.

The decomposition has direct operational consequences. An agent that tries to eliminate *all* mismatch — including the irreducible noise floor — will overfit, adjusting its model to explain noise and degrading future predictions. The update-gain construct introduced next will implicitly separate signal from noise by weighting observations in proportion to their informativeness, but the irreducible-floor fact is what makes the gain question meaningful at all. The decomposition also clarifies the relationship to model sufficiency: when sufficiency is less than 1, the model has lost predictive information relative to the full history; under an alignment assumption (the lost information affects the one-step conditional mean) this implies positive model error in the decomposition.
