# Diagrams, Illustrations & Concept Maps in Monographs, Textbooks, and Theses: A Focused Survey
## Executive Summary
Academic publishing formats — monographs, higher-education textbooks, and dissertations/theses — each impose distinct constraints on illustration design while sharing a common body of evidence about what works. Research across biology, science, mathematics, and cross-disciplinary learning confirms that properly designed visuals are among the most powerful levers available to academic authors. Yet the research also shows that the majority of textbook diagrams, and nearly all thesis figures, fall substantially short of their potential, and that decorative or overcomplex visuals actively harm comprehension. This report synthesizes findings specific to these three publication formats.

***
## Part I: Textbook Illustrations — The State of the Field
### 1.1 The Expanding Role of Visuals in Textbooks
From the 1950s to the 1980s, the proportion of pages in college biology textbooks containing *no* illustration fell from 52% to 22%. In the same period, photographs increased nearly threefold and color became universal. This trend has continued: the fourth edition of Keeton's *Biological Science* had over 75% more page space than the first, but only a 10% increase in words—the majority of added space went to illustrations. Three forces drive this: good artwork sells textbooks; illustrations are effective cognitive devices; and visuals save space by conveying complex information in less room than prose.[^1]

Yet quantity and quality have diverged. Researchers have documented that diagrams within the same textbook, and across competing textbooks aimed at the same audience, vary wildly in complexity with no principled rationale. A 2023 analysis of 749 diagrams across Grade 7–10 science textbooks found that the dominant image function was *classificational* or *analytical*, with relatively few diagrams performing the higher-order interpretational or transformational roles that most accelerate comprehension.[^2][^1]
### 1.2 A Design Revelation: Small Changes, Large Effects
A pivotal 2024 study by Jennifer Landin at NC State, published in *Education Sciences*, enrolled 684 undergraduates and assigned each one of three variants of a life-cycle diagram:[^3]

1. **Traditional circular diagram** with a single offspring
2. **Circular diagram** with multiple offspring
3. **Linear (left-to-right) diagram** with multiple offspring

Results were striking:
- Students with the *multiple-offspring* diagram scored **28–30% higher** on questions about offspring survival than those with the single-offspring version
- Students with the *linear* diagram scored **19–30% higher** on evolution questions than those with the circular version
- The best-performing group (linear + multiple offspring) answered **54.5%** of questions correctly, compared to only **26.1%** for the traditional circular + single-offspring group[^3]

The conclusion: two small design changes — switching the layout direction and showing multiple offspring rather than one — roughly *doubled* student performance. The researchers noted that the circular format, while traditional, implies closure and repetition rather than heritable change across generations, and thus communicates the opposite of what evolutionary reasoning requires. This study stands as a paradigmatic example of how entrenched diagram conventions can silently undermine pedagogical intent.[^3]
### 1.3 Levin's Functional Taxonomy of Textbook Illustrations
The most widely cited classification framework for textbook visuals is Levin, Anglin & Carney's (1987) taxonomy, which groups pictures in prose into five primary functions:[^1][^4][^5]

| Function | Description | Effect on Learning |
|----------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Decorational** | Aesthetically appealing but conceptually irrelevant | Neutral to negative |
| **Representational** | Depicts the concrete object being discussed | Moderate positive |
| **Organizational** | Shows structural/spatial relationships among elements | Strong positive |
| **Interpretational** | Clarifies difficult concepts; helps explain abstract ideas | Strong positive |
| **Transformational** | Encodes material in a memorable, mnemonic form designed for recall | Strongest positive |

The key finding: *decorational* and *representational* illustrations have inconsistent or negligible effects on learning outcomes, while *organizational*, *interpretational*, and *transformational* illustrations reliably improve comprehension and retention. Yet the majority of visuals in commercially published textbooks are of the first two types.[^4][^6][^1]

**The practical implication**: when auditing a textbook's visuals or designing new ones, ask which of these five functions is being served. Any diagram serving only a decorational function should be redesigned to serve at minimum an organizational one.
### 1.4 The Seductive Details Hazard — A Textbook-Specific Problem
The *seductive details effect* is particularly acute in textbooks, which are read at the reader's own pace without the instructor to redirect attention. Seductive details are interesting but conceptually irrelevant elements — interesting photographs, colorful tangential facts, striking visuals unrelated to the core concept.[^7][^8]

The empirical record is unambiguous:
- A meta-analysis across 7,500+ learners found that seductive details *consistently* reduced learning, with effect sizes of **d = 0.95 for retention** and **d = 0.83 for transfer** specifically for seductive *illustrations* (stronger than for seductive text passages)[^8]
- Seductive details harm learning primarily via *diversion* — attention is captured by an irrelevant element, depriving the core concept of processing[^9]
- An eye-tracking study found that learners with *lower inhibitory control* spent significantly longer fixating on seductive illustrations, with the biggest comprehension losses in that group[^10]
- The effect is stronger for **paper-based** textbooks than digital slides, because printed handouts present relevant and irrelevant content simultaneously in large chunks, while slides can segment information[^7]

**Implication for textbook design**: every illustration that is not performing an organizational, interpretational, or transformational function is a candidate for removal. The NC State study, the seductive details meta-analyses, and the Ohio State study on bar graphs with decorative images all point in the same direction: *simplification consistently beats enrichment* for comprehension.[^11]
### 1.5 The NARST 18-Characteristic Framework for Textbook Visuals
The National Association for Research in Science Teaching synthesizes science textbook visual research into 18 empirically grounded characteristics of effective visuals. Key criteria include:[^12]

- **Accurate spatial relations**: visuals must portray correct proportional and relational information
- **Non-distracting**: irrelevant colorful photographs should not compete with conceptual content
- **Illustrate difficult-to-image information**: visuals should be used precisely where prose fails — for processes, spatial relationships, and dynamic sequences
- **Focus students' attention**: arrows, adjunct questions, and other cues guide learner attention to critical features
- **Summarize and contrast**: diagrams should earn their place by condensing comparative or relational information that would require multiple paragraphs in prose
- **Referenced in text**: every visual must be explicitly cross-referenced in the adjacent prose — unreferenced figures are rarely consulted[^12]
- **Facilitate coordinated reading**: the diagram and its text should be spatially arranged so the eye can easily alternate between them without losing track[^12]
- **Orchestrated as an ensemble**: within a chapter, visuals should work together — building on each other and forming a coherent instructional arc[^12]
### 1.6 Diagram Complexity Calibration
One of the most persistent problems in textbooks is the mismatch between a diagram's complexity and the visual literacy of its intended audience. Publishers often include sophisticated computer-generated visuals (3D protein structures, molecular dynamics simulations) that presuppose interpretive skills the audience does not possess. The result is that the diagram becomes decorational by default — the student cannot read it, so it adds no cognitive value.[^1]

Research guidance: the complexity of a diagram should match the cognitive level of the intended reader, and new visual conventions (e.g., the convention for showing motion, depth, or time within a 2D static image) should be explicitly taught before being deployed.[^12][^1]

## Part II: Concept Maps in Higher Education
### 2.1 Quantitative Evidence Base
Concept mapping is the most extensively meta-analyzed visual tool in higher education. The Visible Learning Meta-X database aggregates 16 meta-analyses across 1,442 studies, 36,963 students, and 1,612 effect sizes, yielding a **weighted mean effect size of 0.66** — well above the 0.40 threshold Hattie identifies as "desirable". Recency matters: the most recent meta-analyses consistently show stronger effects than older ones, as concept map tools and techniques have improved.[^13]

Selected meta-analytic effect sizes across the literature:[^13]

| Study | Domain | N Studies | Effect Size (d) |
|-------|--------|-----------|-----------------|
| Izci & Akkoc (2024) | All subjects | 78 studies | **1.08** |
| Wang & Gu (2008) | All subjects | 26 studies | 0.84 |
| Wong (2021) | Collaborative concept mapping | 30 studies | 0.88 |
| Shi et al. (2022) | Mind mapping | 21 studies | 0.99 |
| Schroeder et al. (2018) | Constructing concept maps | 143 studies | 0.59 |
| Nesbit & Adesope (2006) | Concept & knowledge maps | 55 studies | 0.55 |
| Anastasiou et al. (2024) | Science education | 55 studies | 0.78 |

The 2023/2024 PMC meta-analysis by Izci and Akkoc, the most recent at time of writing, found an overall effect of **d = 1.08**, categorized as a *strong* effect on academic achievement.[^14]
### 2.2 The Mechanism: Making Thinking Visible
Concept maps work through multiple mechanisms simultaneously:[^15]
- They require learners to identify *propositions* — explicit relationships between concepts, articulated as a labeled linking phrase ("causes," "leads to," "is a type of") — rather than passively receiving them
- They force learners to encode conceptual structure in a spatial representation that can be compared, revised, and inspected externally
- They activate prior knowledge by requiring learners to connect new concepts to nodes they already understand
- The process of map construction produces *germane cognitive load* — the productive, schema-building kind

Novak and Cañas (2008) explicitly frame concept maps as tools that "make thinking visible" by transforming tacit knowledge into inspectable, structured, external representations.[^15]
### 2.3 Studying vs. Constructing Concept Maps
Schroeder et al.'s (2018) meta-analysis of 143 studies distinguished between learners who *study* pre-built concept maps and those who *construct* their own:[^16][^17]
- Both conditions produced positive learning effects
- The effect of *constructing* maps (d ≈ 0.72) exceeded the effect of *studying* pre-built maps (d ≈ 0.46)
- The longer learners utilized concept maps, the more effective they were — duration matters for cumulative effect[^17]

This has direct implications for textbook design: embedding pre-built concept maps is valuable, but providing learners with map-building tasks using textbook content is more valuable. In the context of dense monographs and thesis-length work, structuring chapter summaries as skeleton concept maps that readers complete would likely increase retention significantly.
### 2.4 Concept Maps for Graduate-Level and Interdisciplinary Content
At graduate and post-graduate level, concept maps are particularly effective for:[^15]
- **Cross-domain synthesis**: mapping how concepts from different disciplines relate to each other (essential for thesis work that spans, e.g., mathematics and philosophy)
- **Formative assessment**: instructors can identify misconceptions by examining which linking propositions students generate
- **Research planning**: concept maps used to plan a dissertation or monograph chapter reveal logical gaps, circular dependencies, and missing evidence before writing begins
- **Literature review organization**: mapping the conceptual landscape of a field before writing a literature review is an established scholarly practice with documented benefits

The *cross-linking* capability of concept maps is uniquely powerful at the graduate level: connections between branches that appear unrelated often represent the most intellectually generative insights in a research project.[^15]

## Part III: Thesis and Dissertation Figures — Conventions, Best Practices, and Evidence
### 3.1 The Structural Role of Figures in Scholarly Argument
In a thesis or dissertation, figures serve a fundamentally different argumentative function than they do in a textbook. A textbook figure explains established knowledge to a learner; a thesis figure supports an original claim to a skeptical expert reader. This distinction generates different design imperatives:[^18]

- Thesis figures must be **self-interpreting**: a reader should be able to understand what the figure shows and why it matters without re-reading the text[^19]
- The caption must work as a complete unit: declarative title (stating the key finding), brief methodological context, and any statistical information needed to evaluate the claim[^20][^19]
- The figure should *support* interpretation, not substitute for it: interpretation remains the text's responsibility[^18]
- The "division of labour" principle: data in visual form, interpretation in prose, minimal redundancy between the two[^18]
### 3.2 Ten Simple Rules for Scientific Figures (Rougier et al., 2014)
Rougier, Droettboom, and Bourne's widely cited 2014 PLOS Computational Biology paper provides ten rules specifically calibrated for academic publication figures:[^21]

1. **Know your audience**: student audiences require more explicit scaffolding than expert colleagues; general-public figures require radical simplification
2. **Identify your message**: every figure should express one central idea; that idea should guide every design decision
3. **Adapt to the medium**: a figure designed for a printed thesis will look different from one designed for a conference presentation; do not reuse without redesign
4. **Captions are not optional**: captions explain how to read the figure and provide precision that cannot be represented graphically
5. **Do not trust the defaults**: software defaults are generic; every element requires manual adjustment for the specific communication goal
6. **Use color effectively**: use color only when it encodes information; avoid rainbow colormaps (perceptually non-linear and not colorblind-safe); distinguish sequential, diverging, and qualitative colormaps
7. **Do not mislead the reader**: rescaled axes, 3D charts, and pie charts systematically distort perception; use the simplest chart type that conveys the message
8. **Avoid chartjunk**: every mark that does not encode information is extraneous load; remove grid lines, colored backgrounds, decorative elements
9. **Message trumps beauty**: a scientifically clear, accurate figure that looks plain outperforms an aesthetically impressive figure that obscures the claim
10. **Get the right tool**: domain-appropriate tools (matplotlib, R/ggplot2, TikZ/PGF, D3.js, Inkscape) produce figures that are reproducible, vector-quality, and modifiable[^21]
### 3.3 Figure Captions: From Descriptive to Declarative
A major shift in academic publishing norms over the past decade concerns the *function* of figure captions. Traditional practice was purely descriptive: "Scatterplot showing the relationship between X and Y." Contemporary best practice, increasingly required by leading journals and recommended for theses, is *declarative*: a caption that states the main finding the figure supports.[^19]

The declarative caption format:
1. **Declarative title**: "Intervention A significantly outperforms the baseline on all three metrics" (not "Comparison of intervention and baseline")
2. **Methods brief**: enough methodological context to interpret the visual without re-reading the methods section
3. **Statistical annotations**: sample sizes, p-values, confidence interval specifications
4. **Abbreviations**: any non-standard abbreviation defined within the caption

The rationale is dual: readers who skim figures and captions before reading body text will immediately grasp the argument's structure; authors who write declarative captions are forced to confirm that each figure makes a single, unambiguous point rather than dumping complex data without committed interpretation.[^19]
### 3.4 Placement, Numbering, and Referencing in Theses
Institutional guidelines converge on several non-negotiable conventions:[^22][^23]

- **Location options**: (a) within the chapter immediately following first reference; (b) grouped at the end of the relevant chapter; (c) grouped at the end of the thesis — whichever is chosen must be applied consistently throughout
- **Numbering**: either sequential throughout (Figure 1, 2, 3...) or chapter-prefixed (Figure 3.1, 3.2...) — the chapter-prefixed system is generally preferred for long documents
- **Resolution and font**: all lettering must be at minimum 2mm high after any reduction for printing; resolution minimum 300dpi for halftones
- **Every figure must be referenced in text**: unreferenced figures are to academic writing what unreferenced citations are — they do not exist argumentatively[^22]
### 3.5 Common Thesis Figure Failure Modes
Academic writing advisors and dissertation coaches identify recurring design failures specific to thesis figures:[^18][^23]

- **The data dump**: a complex, multi-layered figure that presents all collected data without a clear central point — the visual equivalent of presenting raw output; fix by identifying one claim per figure and designing around it
- **Omitted call-out**: the figure is not referenced or discussed in the surrounding text; the reader must guess what it is supposed to show
- **Caption as axis restatement**: the caption merely lists what the axes show rather than explaining what the pattern means; fix by writing declarative captions
- **Inconsistent visual language**: different chapters use different color conventions, symbol sets, or layouts for the same conceptual elements; fix by establishing a visual style guide before drafting
- **Figures without methodological context**: readers cannot evaluate whether the figure supports the claim without knowing basic parameters (n, experimental conditions, error measure used)[^18]

## Part IV: Monograph-Specific Illustration Considerations
### 4.1 The Scholarly Monograph and Its Visual Economy
Academic monographs — the primary publication format in humanities and interpretive social sciences, and still significant in philosophy, mathematics, and CS theory — face a distinctive set of constraints that shape illustration practice:[^24][^25]

- **Argumentative primacy**: the monograph's raison d'être is a sustained, nuanced argument; images serve the argument, not vice versa
- **Cost constraints**: academic presses typically print in black and white; color printing requires a special arrangement and significantly increases per-copy cost[^25]
- **Accessibility requirements**: post-2021 ebook standards require alt text for every image, adding editorial overhead proportional to image count[^25]
- **Readership expertise**: unlike textbooks, monographs assume expert readers who will interrogate, not just absorb, every figure

The University Press of Colorado's figure guidelines articulate the scholarly standard explicitly: authors must be "intentional and judicious" about why images are included and how they serve the book's aims. Unlike textbook production, where images are often added by an art department working from an author's manuscript, monograph illustrations must be authored by the scholar and must carry the argumentative weight the text assigns to them.[^25]
### 4.2 Diagram Types Best Suited to Monographs
Given the black-and-white constraint and the argumentative context, the most effective illustration types for monographs are those that rely on *structure* rather than *color* for their meaning:

- **Argument diagrams** (Toulmin, Wigmore, or box-and-arrow): make the logical skeleton of a chapter's argument explicit and auditable
- **Conceptual taxonomy trees**: show how the author's conceptual framework organizes the domain under study
- **Process and flow diagrams**: clarify the mechanics of a model or theory that would require multiple pages to describe in prose
- **Comparison matrices**: allow the reader to simultaneously see the author's evaluation across multiple criteria
- **Historical/genealogical diagrams**: show intellectual lineage, influence relationships, or the development of a theoretical tradition over time

For monographs in mathematics and logic, diagrams have a further epistemic status: they can serve as contributions to the argument itself, not merely as illustrations of it.[^26][^27]
### 4.3 The JSTOR Principles for Monograph Reader Engagement
The Scholarly Kitchen reported on a set of twelve functional design principles developed by JSTOR for encouraging deep engagement with monographs in digital formats. Central themes include:[^28]

- Creating clear navigational affordances so readers can trace arguments across long texts without losing orientation
- Providing visual summaries (structured concept maps or argument diagrams) that allow scholars to assess the work's logic before committing to close reading
- Enabling granular citation and annotation at the figure level, not just the page level
- Supporting visual *pathways* through an argument that bypass the linear reading sequence for experienced readers who are evaluating rather than learning[^28]

## Part V: Cross-Format Evidence — What the Research Agrees On
Across textbooks, concept maps, and theses, the following findings are consistent and robust:
### 5.1 Multimodal Integration Always Outperforms Single-Mode Presentation
Schnotz and Bannert's Integrated Model of Text and Picture Comprehension (1999/2014) provides the theoretical architecture: learners build both a *propositional text base* (from reading) and a *visual model* (from diagrams), and then construct an *integrating mental model* that draws on both. When text and diagram are coherently co-designed, the resulting mental model is more complete, more flexible, and more durable than one built from either channel alone.[^29][^30][^31]

The 2025 Frontiers study on integrative processing of text and multiple maps found that even for advanced learners, simultaneous presentation of text with two or more related visual representations produced better comprehension than any single representation — but only when the visual representations were semantically aligned and cross-referenced.[^32]
### 5.2 Instructor-Provided vs. Learner-Generated Visuals
A 2025 meta-analysis specifically examined the difference between instructor-provided graphic organizers and learner-generated ones. The headline finding: *learner-generated* visuals produced larger effects on learning than instructor-provided ones — consistent with the earlier finding for concept maps (§2.3). The mechanism is that constructing a visual requires the learner to actively retrieve, organize, and evaluate their understanding, producing richer encoding.[^33]

**Implication for textbook and monograph authors**: providing *partial* diagrams (skeletal frameworks with gaps the reader fills in) outperforms providing *complete* diagrams. For thesis writing, generating one's own concept map before reading each chapter to be reviewed produces better long-term retention than studying provided diagrams.
### 5.3 Diagram Optimization Is Iterative
A 2024 study on graphic organizers optimized with segmenting and signaling principles found that the same base diagram, redesigned with segmentation (breaking the diagram into learner-paced components) and signaling (adding visual cues that highlight structure), produced significantly better comprehension on scientific expository texts. The implication is that diagram design should be treated as an iterative process with explicit testing against the target audience, not as a one-pass authorial decision.[^34]
### 5.4 Visual Literacy Is a Prerequisite, Not a Given
The research consensus is that the *ability to read* complex diagrams is a skill that must be explicitly taught. This is especially true for:[^1][^2]
- Stylized representations of processes at scales outside human experience (cellular, molecular, cosmological)
- Multi-layer diagrams that show the same system at different temporal or scale levels simultaneously
- Visual conventions that are domain-specific (e.g., commutative diagram notation, truth-table layout, formal grammar parse trees)

The practical implication: textbooks, monographs, and theses that deploy specialized visual conventions should include a *visual legend* — not merely a symbol legend, but an explicit explanation of how to read the diagram type itself, analogous to the first time an author uses an unusual notation.

## Summary Matrix: Design Guidelines by Publication Format
| Design Decision | Textbook | Monograph | Thesis/Dissertation |
|-----------------|----------|-----------|---------------------|
| **Primary function** | Explanation and schema-building | Argument support | Claim evidence |
| **Best diagram types** | Interpretational, transformational, organizational | Argument maps, conceptual taxonomies, process diagrams | Data figures, conceptual models, method diagrams |
| **Caption style** | Explanatory + question-prompting | Argumentative + cross-referenced | Declarative (states the finding) |
| **Color use** | Justified if available; semantic only | Typically black-and-white | Check journal/department standards |
| **Complexity calibration** | Match to course level; teach conventions | Assume expert reader; annotate if novel | Match to committee + field readers |
| **Concept maps** | Embed + scaffold construction tasks | Use as chapter navigational summaries | Use in lit review planning and chapter organization |
| **Placement rule** | Adjacent to relevant text (spatial contiguity) | Must follow or precede the text that references it | Immediately following first in-text reference |
| **Redundancy** | Some redundancy acceptable for novices | Avoid: expert readers find it condescending | Avoid: examiners may see it as insufficient analysis |
| **Worst anti-pattern** | Seductive/decorative images | Images that are impressive but unargued | Data dumps with no central claim |
| **Learner-generated visuals** | High value if embedded as tasks | Not applicable | High value for self-comprehension during research |
