## Class A — composite-agent and running-software-as-agent material

### A1. Supervision trees as the industrial implementation of `#der-class-coercion-via-wrapping`

**Source analyses:** [060-supervisor-trees](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/060-supervisor-trees.md), [112-supervision-strategies](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/112-supervision-strategies.md), [089-supervision-lifecycle](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/089-supervision-lifecycle.md), [598-supervision-tree-design-patterns](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/598-supervision-tree-design-patterns---optimal-hierarchical-organization-of-supervisors-and-workers.md), [794-fault-tolerance-patterns](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/794-fault-tolerance-patterns---building-resilient-systems-through-supervision.md), [795-supervisor-tree-design](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/795-supervisor-tree-design---hierarchical-supervision-for-complex-systems.md), [798-supervision-based-reliability](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/798-supervision-based-reliability---using-supervision-for-system-reliability.md), [437-supervision-tree-design](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/437-supervision-tree-design---fault-tolerant-process-hierarchies-in-actor-model-systems.md), [823-fault-isolation-through-supervision-strategy](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/823-fault-isolation-through-supervision-strategy.md), [592-lifecycle-vs-supervision-distinction](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/592-lifecycle-vs-supervision-distinction.md), [594-application-vs-supervisor-startup-orchestration](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/594-application-vs-supervisor-startup-orchestration.md), [223-otp-applications](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/223-otp-applications.md).
**Class:** A
**AAT-relevance:** `#der-class-coercion-via-wrapping`, `#impl-composition-machinery`, `#scope-composite-agent`.

**The content (briefly).** Across ~12 analyses, OTP supervision trees are formalized as a hierarchical structure where each supervisor's role is *exactly* the externally-imposed scaffold that gives the composite the properties its component processes do not individually have. A worker GenServer crashes when it encounters an unexpected state — by itself it has no fault-tolerance property. The supervisor wrapping it commits structurally to: (i) detecting the crash through process linking ($t_f \approx 0$ via the BEAM's monitor primitive), (ii) restoring the worker to its clean initial state $S_0$ in roughly microseconds, (iii) preserving state outside the worker (the *stash* pattern — analysis 060 names this and observes that $O(n_{\text{state elements}})$ recovery collapses to $O(1)$ when state is held in a stash process). The supervisor itself is also a process under a higher supervisor, so the property holds at each level by induction. Analysis 060: $P(\text{system-survives}) \approx 1 - (\text{failure-rate})^d$ for depth-$d$ tree — system reliability grows *exponentially* with supervision depth while comprehension complexity grows only *logarithmically* (analysis 598).

**Translation into AAT/TST.** This is the cleanest concrete instantiation of `#der-class-coercion-via-wrapping` we have. Each GenServer worker is, in isolation, a Class 3 candidate — it has goal-conditioned state, and a corrupted message can drive its internal $M_t$ into a state from which no goal-blind belief-update query will recover. The supervisor wrapper *coerces* the composite to Class 1: by structural commitment of *terminate-and-reinitialize on detected mismatch*, the composite agent's $M_t$ update at the wrapper level is guaranteed goal-blind (it does not consult $O_t$ or $\Sigma_t$ — it just executes the restart strategy specified at wire-up time). The wrapper's strategy DAG is fixed at compile time and does not adapt; the composite achieves Class-1 directed separation by paying a leakage cost in the form of lost ephemeral state (any $S_{\text{ephemeral}}$ held by the crashed worker is gone) plus restart-induced tempo overhead. This is the W₁ regime — structural commitment, structural leakage bound. The stash-process pattern is a refinement that reduces the leakage rate by externalizing the *essential* state subset $S_{\text{essential}}$ to a more-stable wrapper-co-resident process, at the cost of additional wire-up complexity. **Land in:** strengthens `#der-class-coercion-via-wrapping` with a concrete W₁ realization; suggests adding a *Worked example: OTP supervision as W₁* discussion paragraph or a chapter-end implications row in `#impl-composition-machinery`. Could also land in a new TST segment in the "composite developer agent" chapter — *Supervision as wrapper-induced class coercion*.

**Honesty.** The analyses cleanly capture the *mechanism* (crash → restart → clean state, with stash for essential state) but do not have AAT's vocabulary for what the wrapper achieves *structurally* — they describe it as fault-tolerance, not as a coercion of GUC class. The mathematical formalization is informal (e.g., $A_{\text{supervised}} = 1 - (1 - A_{\text{component}})^n$ assumes independent failures, which is exactly what restart-intensity loops violate — analysis 060 acknowledges this in its counter-examples). The "nine nines" claim is hyperbolic and the analyses recognize it. The cleanest theoretical claim — that supervision tree depth $d$ gives $P(\text{survive}) \approx 1 - \lambda^d$ — is conditional on independent failures and on the supervisor itself being stable, which is exactly the recursive property the wrapping construction needs and exactly where the leakage-rate bound has to be made precise.

### A2. Three restart strategies (`:one_for_one` / `:one_for_all` / `:rest_for_one`) as a W₁ regime *taxonomy*

**Source analyses:** [112-supervision-strategies](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/112-supervision-strategies.md), [823-fault-isolation-through-supervision-strategy](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/823-fault-isolation-through-supervision-strategy.md), [437-supervision-tree-design](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/437-supervision-tree-design---fault-tolerant-process-hierarchies-in-actor-model-systems.md), [598-supervision-tree-design-patterns](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/598-supervision-tree-design-patterns---optimal-hierarchical-organization-of-supervisors-and-workers.md), [594-application-vs-supervisor-startup-orchestration](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/594-application-vs-supervisor-startup-orchestration.md).
**Class:** A
**AAT-relevance:** `#der-class-coercion-via-wrapping` (refinement — the wrapper choice has a structure-of-coupling parameter not currently surfaced).

**The content (briefly).** A supervisor has to choose, at wire-up time, what to do when a child crashes. The three canonical choices encode different *failure-coupling* commitments: `:one_for_one` restarts only the failed child (children treated as independent), `:one_for_all` restarts everyone (children treated as a single unit — used for processes that share a consensus invariant or a startup ordering that requires all-or-nothing), `:rest_for_one` restarts the failed child plus everyone started after it (children form a directed pipeline where downstream consumers depend on upstream producers' state). Analysis 112 gives the SCC-based selection algorithm: compute strongly-connected components of the dependency graph; singleton-SCCs → `:one_for_one`; one big SCC → `:one_for_all`; chain-of-SCCs → `:rest_for_one`. Analysis 823 formalizes the resulting alignment score as $\text{alignment} = 1 - n_p^{-2}\sum_{i,j}\lvert\rho_{ij}^{(\text{semantic})} - \rho_{ij}^{(\text{strategy})}\rvert$, where $\rho^{(\text{strategy})}$ is the failure coupling the strategy *imposes* and $\rho^{(\text{semantic})}$ is what the domain *requires*.

**Translation into AAT/TST.** The restart strategy is a *structural commitment* about which sub-agents the wrapper treats as separable. Each strategy realizes a different W-regime *sub-mode* of the same W₁ construction: it sets the granularity at which the wrapper's "reset to clean state" operation acts. Mapping: `:one_for_one` = maximal separation (each child is its own restart unit), the cleanest structural commitment but only sound when children are conceptually independent; `:one_for_all` = group restart, accepts higher tempo cost (per `#der-tempo-composition` — Brooks's Law) in exchange for enforcing an *all-or-nothing* invariant across the children (this is the class coercion of a *consensus-like* sub-agent — a tribunal/quorum is fundamentally `:one_for_all`); `:rest_for_one` = ordered partial restart, the wrapper's failure-coupling matches a directed-pipeline DAG. This is the operational substrate for the project's broader composition language: choosing the supervision strategy *is* the act of declaring the composite's strategy-DAG structure at the failure-handling layer. **Land in:** an addition to `#der-class-coercion-via-wrapping` that names the *wrapper structural-coupling* parameter — a one-page worked example would show how the three OTP strategies map onto three points in the wrapping-construction's design space, all W₁ but with different residual-leakage profiles. Possibly a new appendix segment *Wrapping sub-modes: how the wrapper's failure-coupling choice encodes the composite's intended strategy DAG*.

**Honesty.** The analyses present these strategies as engineering choices, not as structural commitments in the formal sense. The mapping "strategy = imposed failure coupling" is real but the analyses don't make the AAT connection. The alignment-score formula in 823 is suggestive but not derivable from a clean axiomatization — it's the right shape but the constants and weighting are heuristic. The *strategic* point is that this taxonomy already exists, battle-tested across thousands of production BEAM systems for two decades, and exhausts the natural choices at this level of abstraction. AAT did not invent it; AAT clarifies *what it is* (a parametrization of the W₁ wrapping construction).

### A3. Restart-intensity / max-restarts as the wrapper's *strategic persistence* bound

**Source analyses:** [060-supervisor-trees](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/060-supervisor-trees.md), [112-supervision-strategies](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/112-supervision-strategies.md), [598-supervision-tree-design-patterns](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/598-supervision-tree-design-patterns---optimal-hierarchical-organization-of-supervisors-and-workers.md), [597-child-specification-optimization](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/597-child-specification-optimization---optimal-patterns-for-defining-child-process-specifications.md), [828-process-health-monitoring-systems](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/828-process-health-monitoring-systems---handling-down-messages-and-process-lifecycle-tracking.md).
**Class:** A — directly answers Joseph's "what is $\mathcal T$ for a running service?" question.
**AAT-relevance:** `#result-persistence-condition` applied at the wrapper level; the bridge from AAT persistence to running-service availability.

**The content (briefly).** OTP supervisors have two parameters that govern when the wrapper *itself* gives up: `max_restarts` and `max_seconds`. The semantics: if more than `max_restarts` child crashes occur within `max_seconds`, the supervisor itself crashes and escalates the failure to its own parent supervisor. Defaults are 3 restarts in 5 seconds — battle-tested across two decades of production BEAM. Analysis 112 gives the design rule $\text{max-restarts} = \lceil \lambda \cdot \text{window} \cdot \text{safety-factor} \rceil$ where $\lambda$ is the observed historical failure rate and the safety factor is $\approx 1.5$. The point: a wrapper that restarts too aggressively masks a real fault (the child has a deterministic bug and will keep crashing the same way — restarting it is just chain-of-failure amplification); a wrapper that gives up too quickly fails to actually coerce the class.

**Translation into AAT/TST.** This is *exactly* the persistence condition $\mathcal T \gt \rho/\lVert\delta_{\text{critical}}\rVert$ specialized to the wrapper's reset-to-clean operation: $\mathcal T_{\text{wrapper}} = 1/\text{restart-time}$ (the wrapper's tempo to detect-and-reset is the inverse restart time, microseconds on BEAM), $\rho_{\text{child}}$ is the child failure rate, and $\lVert\delta_{\text{critical}}\rVert$ is the per-failure damage tolerance — the threshold above which $S_{\text{ephemeral}}$ loss becomes operationally unacceptable. The `max_restarts/max_seconds` envelope encodes the *strategic-persistence* boundary: outside that envelope, the wrapper's class coercion construction *no longer holds* — the child's behavior has crossed a regime boundary that makes the W₁ commitment unsound, and the wrapper escalates rather than continuing to apply a coercion construction that has lost its grounding. **Land in:** worked example for `#scope-continuous-operation` (currently the TST segment that names $P(\text{fail}) \times T_{\text{recovery}}$ as part of the optimization target) and a new candidate TST segment *Persistence condition for the running service*, which would close the OUTLINE Ch.4 gap *Software persistence: the unmaintainability threshold formalized*. This is the running-service analog Joseph specifically asked for: "$\rho$ for a microservice" = child failure rate; "$\eta^\ast$ for an auto-scaler" needs a separate spike but the supervisor's restart-intensity bound is the *strategic* persistence bound at the wrapper level.

**Honesty.** The bridge from the persistence inequality to restart-intensity is genuine but not trivial — `max_restarts` is a count-in-window, not a rate, and the conversion requires care about what counts as "critical disturbance." The deeper claim — that the supervisor escalation behavior *is* the wrapping construction giving up when its coercion guarantee no longer holds — is structurally right but needs a careful argument in a segment, not just a heuristic mapping. This is theorem-grade material once the bridge is tightened: the persistence-condition specialization at the wrapper level should give a precise inequality determining when the W₁ commitment is sustainable under a given failure-rate distribution.

### A4. The "stash" pattern as W₀↔W₁ regime separation through state externalization

**Source analyses:** [060-supervisor-trees](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/060-supervisor-trees.md), [089-supervision-lifecycle](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/089-supervision-lifecycle.md), [112-supervision-strategies](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/112-supervision-strategies.md), [124-genserver-state-management](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/124-genserver-state-management.md), [586-genserver-state-management-patterns](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/586-genserver-state-management-patterns.md).
**Class:** A
**AAT-relevance:** `#der-class-coercion-via-wrapping` (refinement — *how the wrapper preserves what it cannot afford to lose*); intersects with $S_{\text{essential}}$ / $S_{\text{ephemeral}}$ decomposition.

**The content (briefly).** The bare let-it-crash pattern loses all worker state on restart. The *stash process pattern* (analysis 060) separates a worker into two co-located processes: a `Worker` that holds ephemeral processing state and crashes freely, and a `Stash` that holds essential state and is supervised with a much *lower* restart intensity (the rationale: the stash should almost never need to restart; if it does, the supervisor should escalate quickly because the essential state's home being unstable means the whole composite has crossed an unsoundness boundary). The supervisor wires them together with `:rest_for_one` so a Stash crash also restarts the Worker (which depends on the Stash), but a Worker crash does not touch the Stash. Analyses 089 and 112 generalize: state is decomposed into $S_{\text{essential}}$ (must persist across restarts — user data, counters, configuration) and $S_{\text{ephemeral}}$ (can be lost — caches, in-flight buffers, performance metrics); the essential set is moved to a separate, more-stable process; the worker becomes recoverable in $O(1)$ time.

**Translation into AAT/TST.** This is the operational substrate for distinguishing structural leakage (W₁) from behavioral leakage (W₂) inside the wrapping construction: by placing $S_{\text{essential}}$ in a separately-supervised sub-component with a tighter persistence guarantee, the wrapper achieves *structural* preservation of essential state across worker resets (W₁ regime for the essential subset), while accepting *behavioral* loss of ephemeral state on each reset (the ephemeral subset is in W₂ — bounded by the failure rate times the volume of ephemeral state lost per failure). The stash construction is the worked example for how a wrapper *splits its own state* across multiple regime sub-domains. **Land in:** strengthens `#der-class-coercion-via-wrapping` with the structural-vs-behavioral split as a *partition* on the wrapped state rather than as two competing wrapper designs. Worth a short discussion paragraph in `#impl-composition-machinery` or as a new sub-result in the wrapping segment: *State partition under wrapping — essential subset in W₁, ephemeral subset in W₂*.

**Honesty.** The pattern is widely deployed and the cost-benefit analysis is sound. The harder part for AAT is the *categorization rule* — what counts as "essential" is a domain judgment, not a property derivable from the agent's structure. Joseph's broader framing (M4 modularity-state-dynamics) suggests this categorization is itself under pressure from adversarial coupling — what looks ephemeral can become essential as the composite's strategic exposure changes. A clean theory would derive the partition from a value-function on state subsets, not just take it as given. That is real work, but it's exactly the kind of theory-side yield AAT could provide that the analyses cannot.

### A5. Lightweight processes (roughly 1–5 microsecond spawn cost) as the structural enabler of the wrapping construction

**Source analyses:** [030-actor-model-processes](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/030-actor-model-processes.md), [777-process-overhead-and-actor-model-scalability](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/777-process-overhead-and-actor-model-scalability.md), [773-process-creation-patterns](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/773-process-creation-patterns---spawning-lightweight-processes-efficiently.md), [774-process-overhead-management](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/774-process-overhead-management---understanding-performance-characteristics-of-processes.md), [780-process-isolation-principles](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/780-process-isolation-principles---ensuring-process-independence-and-safety.md).
**Class:** A
**AAT-relevance:** `#der-class-coercion-via-wrapping` (the cost side); `#der-tempo-composition`.

**The content (briefly).** Across multiple analyses: BEAM processes have roughly 5-microsecond spawn cost on 2011 hardware (Thomas's million-process benchmark in roughly 5s) — orders of magnitude below OS processes (ms) or OS threads (hundreds of microseconds). The behavioral consequence is what Thomas calls a *design-level* change, not just a performance one: developers create processes "as casually as they would create objects in Java" (analyses 030, 777). The analyses observe but do not formalize the deeper structural point: process spawn cost being lower than the *human comprehension cost of state-sharing* changes which abstraction is the path of least resistance.

**Translation into AAT/TST.** The W₁ wrapping construction has a tempo cost — Brooks's Law on the wrapper means each wrapped sub-agent adds overhead, and the wrapping construction only pays off if the wrapper's structural commitment buys enough class coercion to offset the overhead. On the BEAM, the *wrapper-instantiation cost* is negligible — spawning a process is cheaper than the structural-coupling cost of *not* spawning one. This makes the BEAM/OTP environment a *limiting case* for `#der-class-coercion-via-wrapping`: wrappers can be made finer-grained than they would be in any other production environment without violating the tempo-composition inequality. The cost coefficient in $\mathcal T_{\text{composite}} \leq \sum_i \mathcal T_i \cdot (1 - \text{overhead}_i)$ is dominated by the *human* coordination overhead (defining message protocols, debugging supervision hierarchies), not by the runtime process-spawn cost. **Land in:** a worked-example slot under `#der-tempo-composition` — *BEAM as a limiting case where wrapper instantiation tempo cost approaches zero* — and a calibration note for the chapter on running-software-as-agent. The bridge to the developer agent's $\mathcal T_{\text{dev}}$ is direct: BEAM moves the dominant tempo cost from runtime to comprehension, which is the developer agent's bottleneck, not the runtime's.

**Honesty.** The 5-microsecond figure is for cold-start no-state spawn; real supervised processes with initialization callbacks (`init/1`, state hydration from stash) are more expensive but still microsecond-scale. The "1 million processes in 5s" benchmark is sequential spawn, not parallel — the parallel number is higher. Importantly, the analyses conflate *process* spawn cost with *wrapping* cost; the wrapping construction's full cost includes the human cost of writing the supervisor child-spec, the message protocol, and the supervision-strategy choice, which the analyses count separately as "implementation time." A clean AAT treatment would separate runtime tempo cost (microseconds, negligible) from coordination tempo cost (Brooks's Law in the developer agent's $\mathcal T_{\text{explore}}$ channel).

### A6. The running BEAM service as `#scope-running-software-agent` — concrete instantiation

**Source analyses:** [223-otp-applications](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/223-otp-applications.md), [124-genserver-state-management](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/124-genserver-state-management.md), [088-genserver-boundaries](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/088-genserver-boundaries.md), [149-genserver-callbacks](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/149-genserver-callbacks.md), [589-api-layer-design-patterns](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/589-api-layer-design-patterns---creating-clean-functional-apis-that-hide-genserver-complexity.md), [787-otp-callback-patterns](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/787-otp-callback-patterns---implementing-required-behavior-callbacks.md), [789-server-interface-design](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/789-server-interface-design---clean-api-design-for-server-processes.md), [796-process-lifecycle-management](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/796-process-lifecycle-management---understanding-process-birth-life-and-death.md).
**Class:** A — direct answer to Joseph's named gap on running-software-as-agent.
**AAT-relevance:** new TST segment (`#scope-running-software-agent` or similar — the running-service analog of `#scope-developer-agent`).

**The content (briefly).** GenServer's callback structure (analysis 124) is, structurally, an adaptive-agent state machine: `init/1` constructs the initial $M_t = S_0$; `handle_call/3` and `handle_cast/2` process synchronous and asynchronous messages (observations) and return new state (a Bayesian update); `handle_info/2` processes unsolicited events (environment observations); `terminate/2` is the agent's shutdown procedure. The full callback specification $\delta: S \times M \to S \times R$ in analysis 124 is the same structural shape as a TST-style $(M_t, O_t, \Sigma_t)$ agent at one timescale. Analyses 088, 589, 789 add the *API layer* discipline: a clean external API hides the GenServer's internal state, presenting only the action class the running agent exposes to its environment.

**Translation into AAT/TST.** This is exactly the missing TST segment Joseph named — *the running software system as itself a lower-form agent*. The running BEAM service has: $M_t$ = the GenServer's state (the data structure threaded through callbacks); $\Omega_t$ = the message inbox + external system inputs; $O_t$ = the SLO / correctness invariant the service is designed to maintain (uptime, latency budget, throughput floor — what tells the supervisor when to escalate); $\Sigma_t$ = the dispatch logic (which callback handles which message type, and what state transition follows); $h$ = the observation function (which messages are visible, which are dropped or queued); action class 4 (environment modification) = the GenServer's outgoing messages and side effects. The persistence condition becomes operationally: $\mathcal T_{\text{service}} \gt \rho_{\text{traffic}}/\lVert\delta_{\text{SLO}}\rVert$ — the service's adaptive tempo (its rate of correct state updates per unit time) must exceed the traffic rate times the per-message damage if the message is mishandled. **Land in:** new TST segment `#scope-running-software-agent` paralleling `#scope-developer-agent`, addressing the OUTLINE-named gap *"Discussion + missing —GAP— at top of Ch.4"*. The chapter could be retitled and the segment placed at the chapter head, with the supervision-tree material from A1–A5 as the substrate.

**Honesty.** The mapping is structurally clean but the analyses present GenServer as a Java-object-analog, not as an agent — they recognize the structural analogy without taking it seriously as a theory commitment. The harder question, which the analyses do not address but TST should: *under what conditions does the running service have genuine $G_t$ and not just a fixed dispatch table?* A vanilla GenServer with hard-coded `handle_call` clauses has no $\Sigma_t$ adaptation — it is closer to a Tier-1 reflex than a Tier-3 agent. The interesting cases are services with *adaptive* dispatch (rate limiters that adjust throttling based on observed load; circuit breakers that change state based on failure rate; auto-scalers that update their replica count based on observed throughput) — these are the cases where the service genuinely instantiates the actuated-agent machinery. The mining material gives the substrate but does not draw the line; that line is the segment's content to add. This is real theory work and is also the cleanest fresh contribution AAT can make to OTP-pattern descriptions.

### A7. Let-it-crash as the *temporal optimality* (`#post-temporal-optimality`) restated for the running service

**Source analyses:** [017-let-it-crash](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/017-let-it-crash.md), [102-let-it-crash-philosophy](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/102-let-it-crash-philosophy.md), [433-error-recovery-vs-failure](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/433-error-recovery-vs-failure---when-to-recover-vs-when-to-fail-and-crash-early-philosophy.md), [020-fail-fast](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/020-fail-fast.md).
**Class:** A
**AAT-relevance:** `#scope-continuous-operation`, `#post-temporal-optimality`, the candidate `#scope-running-software-agent` from A6.

**The content (briefly).** The let-it-crash philosophy (analyses 017, 102) is the explicit operational choice to *minimize* $T_{\text{recovery}}$ rather than *minimize* $P(\text{failure})$. Defensive programming tries to make $P(\text{failure}) \to 0$ by checking every precondition and handling every exception path. Let-it-crash accepts $P(\text{failure}) \gt 0$ and engineers $T_{\text{recovery}}$ down toward the microsecond range. Analysis 017: $A_{\text{system}} = \mu / (\lambda + \mu) \to 1$ as restart rate $\mu \gg$ failure rate $\lambda$. Analysis 102 makes the comprehension argument too — defensive programming creates discontinuities at every error boundary ($\sim n_e \cdot t_d$ where $n_e$ is the number of error paths), while let-it-crash creates discontinuities only at supervisor boundaries (a small constant $k$).

**Translation into AAT/TST.** The TST scope segment `#scope-continuous-operation` already names the optimization target as $P(\text{fail}) \times T_{\text{recovery}}$. Let-it-crash is the explicit philosophical commitment that, *given the choice*, the term $T_{\text{recovery}}$ is the right one to minimize because $P(\text{fail}) = 0$ is unattainable under bounded resources and adversarial environments. This is the *operational corollary* of `#post-temporal-optimality` for the running-service regime: the time-optimal recovery strategy is the one that drives the recovery half of the product down faster than the prevention half can be driven down. The analyses' comprehension argument (defensive programming creates exponentially-many discontinuities) is the same shape as the developer-tempo argument in `#der-code-quality-as-observation-infrastructure` — defensive code raises the developer's $U_o$ on the error-handling region of the codebase, lowering effective $\eta^\ast$ on that region, propagating back to $\mathcal T_{\text{dev}}$. **Land in:** strengthens `#scope-continuous-operation` with the *philosophical-choice* framing (named as "let-it-crash" with original attribution to Joe Armstrong / the BEAM tradition, per the prior-art-integration discipline); strengthens the candidate `#scope-running-software-agent` from A6 by giving it an explicit operational stance.

**Honesty.** The analyses present let-it-crash as universally optimal; the analyses themselves admit edge cases (analysis 102: external system boundaries, financial transactions, user-facing errors) where defensive validation is required. ### C2. Compile-time data-to-code generation as a $\Sigma_t$-vs-$M_t$ partition decision

**Source analyses:** [093-compile-time-code-generation](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/093-compile-time-code-generation.md), [094-ast-walking-patterns](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/094-ast-walking-patterns.md).
**Class:** C
**AAT-relevance:** New segment candidate; intersects `#def-strategy-dag` and the question of static-vs-dynamic strategy structure.

**The content (briefly).** Elixir's Unicode handling (analysis 093) reads `UnicodeData.txt` at compile time and generates ~27,000 pattern-matching function clauses — each Unicode codepoint becomes its own function clause for fast pattern-match dispatch. The alternative — runtime table lookup — would be slower. The decision is whether to commit to a *fixed* strategy structure at compile time (cheap dispatch, expensive to change) or keep the strategy mutable at runtime (flexible, slower).

**Translation into AAT/TST.** This is a $\Sigma_t$-vs-$M_t$ partition decision at the language layer. Compile-time generation moves a piece of what would be *runtime-mutable* state into *compile-time-fixed* strategy DAG structure. The trade-off — flexibility versus tempo — has clean AAT vocabulary in `#der-tempo-composition` but is not currently surfaced for the *strategy-structure-versus-state* axis. **Land in:** spike-worthy — does AAT have machinery for the static-vs-dynamic strategy-structure axis? If not, this is a candidate Class C contribution.

**Honesty.** Suggestive but not yet a finding. The analyses present compile-time-vs-runtime as a performance trade-off; the structural reading is mine and might or might not survive scrutiny.

### C3. Property-based testing as Level-2 interventional access on the running system

**Source analyses:** [107-property-based-testing](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/107-property-based-testing.md), [126-property-based-testing](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/126-property-based-testing.md), [078-property-based-testing](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/078-property-based-testing.md).
**Class:** C / B
**AAT-relevance:** `#obs-software-epistemic-properties` (P2 — interventional access via tests).

**The content (briefly).** Property-based testing (PropEr, StreamData) generates random inputs satisfying a generator specification and checks an invariant. On failure, it shrinks the counter-example to the minimal-input form. The point: it explores the input-space *interventionally* in a way that example-based tests do not — it samples from a generator distribution rather than picking individual cases.

**Translation into AAT/TST.** This is a refinement of P2 in `#obs-software-epistemic-properties`. Standard example-based tests give Level-2 interventional access at specific input points; property-based tests give Level-2 access *across* a generator-defined input distribution, more closely approximating the do-calculus $do(X = \cdot)$ over a range. The shrinking step is informative: it identifies the *minimal-counterexample* boundary of the property's domain of validity. **Land in:** worked-example footnote in `#obs-software-epistemic-properties` distinguishing example-based from property-based test access.

**Honesty.** Real distinction but small finding. The property-vs-example axis is well-known; the AAT-Level-2-refinement framing is mine and modest.

### D1. BEAM's "nine nines" telecom uptime as empirical anchor for the persistence-condition regime

**Source analyses:** [060-supervisor-trees](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/060-supervisor-trees.md), [017-let-it-crash](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/017-let-it-crash.md), [102-let-it-crash-philosophy](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/102-let-it-crash-philosophy.md), [223-otp-applications](../../../_core/tst/planning/analysis/223-otp-applications.md).

**AAT-relevance:** `#scope-continuous-operation`; the candidate `#scope-running-software-agent`.

**The content (briefly).** The Ericsson AXD-301 telecom switch, written in Erlang/OTP, is reported to have achieved 99.9999999% (nine-nines) uptime over its operational lifetime. The number is widely quoted, including in Joe Armstrong's PhD thesis. Analyses 060 and 017 cite the figure as empirical evidence for the supervised-actor architecture.

**Translation into AAT/TST.** This is an empirical-anchor data point for the persistence-condition regime in production-deployed software. If a running service's persistence condition holds robustly under the BEAM/OTP wrapping construction at the demonstrated rate, that constrains the parameter space of $\rho_{\text{traffic}} / \mathcal T_{\text{service}}$ that real services can occupy. **Land in:** empirical-anchor citation in `#scope-continuous-operation` or the new running-service-agent segment.

**Honesty.** The "nine nines" figure is widely-quoted but lightly-sourced — it appears in Joe Armstrong's "Making Reliable Distributed Systems in the Presence of Software Errors" (2003) citing AXD-301 deployment data, but the underlying measurement methodology is not publicly available. As a precise number it should be treated as hyperbolic-but-directionally-correct; as evidence that the supervised-actor architecture *can* achieve high uptime in production, it is solid. The analyses' "nine nines $= 1 - \lambda^d$" derivation is a model, not a measurement. Cite as "high uptime reported in production telecom systems" rather than as a specific number.

## Synthesis observation — the BEAM/OTP world as a *retroactively validating* worked-example zone for AAT

The strongest meta-observation from this mining slice: BEAM/OTP is two decades of production engineering converging on patterns that match the structure AAT axiomatizes from first principles. The wrapping construction, the W₀/W₁/W₂ regime hierarchy, the tempo-composition inequality, the structural-vs-behavioral leakage distinction, the persistence condition at the wrapper level, the directed-separation discipline, the goal-blind belief-update commitment — *all of these have battle-tested operational analogs in OTP*. This is not because the BEAM designers were doing AAT; they were doing telecom reliability engineering in the 1980s. It is because the structural problem AAT axiomatizes (how do you build a reliable composite from less-reliable components, while preserving certain class-of-agent properties at the composite layer?) has a unique-up-to-naming solution shape that any sufficiently-mature production engineering tradition will rediscover under different vocabulary. The Erlang/OTP tradition rediscovered it under the names *supervision, linking, monitoring, let-it-crash, application*. AAT's contribution is *naming what they are structurally* and *deriving what they have to be* from first principles — which has the practical effect of letting an engineer reason about new wrapping problems (LLM-developer agents, multi-agent AI compositions, distributed-AI ensembles) by transferring the discipline rather than rediscovering it.

This is a Class C *meta-observation*, not a finding. But it is also the strongest argument for landing the supervision-tree material as worked examples in `#der-class-coercion-via-wrapping` and its neighbors: the structural alignment is too clean to be coincidence, and the analyses' two-decade tradition is the kind of empirical anchor that AAT can deploy against skeptics who view the wrapping construction as armchair theory.
