# Result: Mismatch Decomposition

The first named *result* of the volume. The expected squared mismatch $\mathbb{E}[\Vert\delta_t\Vert^2]$ decomposes cleanly into three additive parts: an **excess term** (the gap between the model's predictive mean $\hat o_t$ and the Bayes predictor $\hat o_t^{\mathrm B} = \mathbb{E}[o_t \mid \mathcal C_{t-1}, a_{t-1}]$ — the best any chronica-measurable model could do), a **state-and-law floor** (the variance the true conditional mean $\bar o_t = \mathbb{E}[o_t \mid S_t, a_{t-1}]$ retains given the interaction history, where $S_t = (\Omega_t, \theta)$ is the environment's state together with its law — untouchable by better modeling; its state part is movable by *acting* to make the history more informative, and its law part shrinks as history accumulates), and a **channel-noise term** (the conditional variance of the observation channel itself, given state, law and action). The result is a Pythagorean refinement of the bias-variance decomposition applied to the prediction problem; all cross-terms vanish — one pair under the fresh-noise global assumption GA-1 (observation noise conditionally independent of the past chronica given the current state, the law and the action), the standard assumption in state-space models, and the third because the Bayes predictor is by construction the conditional mean given the history. The model can improve the first term; acting and accumulating history can shrink the second; the third is a property of the channel.

The conceptual stakes of this seemingly mechanical decomposition are large. The result establishes that prediction error is *structurally persistent* in any realistic adaptive regime — there are floors below which mismatch cannot be driven by any amount of better modeling. The channel floor is set by the observation sensor itself. The state-and-law floor is set by how much of the environment's state and law the interaction history pins down: it binds *every* chronica-measurable predictor — including the Bayes-optimal one — whenever residual uncertainty about them moves the one-step conditional mean. Deterministic, noiseless, perfectly-specified systems are limiting edge cases, not the typical adaptive regime. The total expected squared mismatch is therefore strictly positive whenever any of the three terms is — and in typical settings all three are.

The decomposition has direct operational consequences picked up downstream. An agent that tries to eliminate *all* mismatch — including the two floors — will overfit, adjusting its model to explain noise and degrading future predictions. The update-gain construct #emp-update-gain implicitly separates signal from noise by weighting observations in proportion to their informativeness, but the floor facts are what make the gain question meaningful at all. The decomposition also clarifies the relationship to #def-model-sufficiency: when $S(M_t) \lt 1$, the model has lost predictive information relative to the full history; under an alignment assumption (the lost information affects the one-step conditional mean) this implies a positive estimation term in the decomposition.

## Formal Expression

*[Derived (result-mismatch-decomposition)]*

For any agent-environment pair within AAT's scope ( #scope-adaptive-system), with $S_t = (\Omega_t, \theta)$ the typed epistemic target of #scope-adaptive-system, $\bar o_t = \mathbb{E}[o_t \mid S_t, a_{t-1}]$ the true conditional mean, and $\hat o_t^{\mathrm B} = \mathbb{E}[o_t \mid \mathcal C_{t-1}, a_{t-1}]$ the Bayes predictor (the best predictive mean any chronica-measurable model can produce):

$$\mathbb{E}[\Vert\delta_t\Vert^2] = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}[\Vert\hat{o}_t - \hat o_t^{\mathrm B}\Vert^2]}_{\text{(i) excess over the Bayes predictor (reducible by modeling)}} + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Var}(\bar o_t \mid \mathcal C_{t-1}, a_{t-1})]}_{\text{(ii) state-and-law floor (irreducible by modeling)}} + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Var}(o_t \mid S_t, a_{t-1})]}_{\text{(iii) channel noise (irreducible)}} \gt 0$$

with positivity whenever any term is positive. Grouping (i)+(ii) recovers the coarser bias-variance form $\mathbb{E}[\Vert\hat o_t - \bar o_t\Vert^2] + \mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Var}(o_t \mid S_t, a_{t-1})]$; the refinement shows that the grouped "model error" is bounded below by the state-and-law floor — no chronica-measurable model reduces it to zero. When the law is known, $S_t$ reduces to $\Omega_t$ and every term takes its state-only form.

### Derivation

1. Term (ii) can be positive only if $S_t$ is not determined by $(\mathcal C_{t-1}, a_{t-1})$. For a loop in the adaptive scope ( #scope-adaptive-system) the history leaves residual uncertainty about $S_t$ (its item (a)), and since $\mathcal C_{t-1}$ is coarser than $\mathcal C_t$, the uncertainty at prediction time is at least as large. The identity itself does not use the scope; the scope is what makes term (ii) generically positive (step 4).
2. By #form-agent-model, the model generates predictions $\hat o_t = \mathbb{E}[o_t \mid M_{t-1}, a_{t-1}]$; $M_{t-1}$ is built from the chronica, so $\hat o_t$ is one of the chronica-measurable predictors over which $\hat o_t^{\mathrm B}$ is optimal. By the tower property and GA-1, $\hat o_t^{\mathrm B} = \mathbb{E}[\bar o_t \mid \mathcal C_{t-1}, a_{t-1}]$.
3. Write $\delta_t = (o_t - \bar o_t) + (\bar o_t - \hat o_t^{\mathrm B}) + (\hat o_t^{\mathrm B} - \hat o_t)$ and expand. All three cross-terms vanish by orthogonality (uncorrelated — not independence). The two involving the noise difference: condition on $(S_t, a_{t-1}, \mathcal C_{t-1})$; the other two differences are then fixed, and $\mathbb{E}[o_t - \bar o_t \mid S_t, a_{t-1}, \mathcal C_{t-1}] = \mathbb{E}[o_t - \bar o_t \mid S_t, a_{t-1}] = 0$ by definition of $\bar o_t$ and the fresh-noise assumption GA-1 ($\varepsilon_t$ conditionally independent of $\mathcal C_{t-1}$ given $(S_t, a_{t-1})$). The remaining cross-term: condition on $(\mathcal C_{t-1}, a_{t-1})$; the third difference is fixed, and $\mathbb{E}[\bar o_t - \hat o_t^{\mathrm B} \mid \mathcal C_{t-1}, a_{t-1}] = 0$ by step 2.
4. Term (iii) is positive when observation noise is non-degenerate. Term (ii) is positive exactly when residual uncertainty about $S_t$ *moves the one-step conditional mean* — step 1's residual uncertainty is necessary for this but not sufficient (uncertainty confined to mean-irrelevant coordinates, or to higher moments only, leaves the floor at zero); this is the same alignment qualifier used for the sufficiency connection in the Discussion. Term (i) is positive when the model's predictive mean differs from the Bayes predictor. Any one suffices.

## Epistemic Status

*Exact* under the fresh-noise assumption (observation noise $\varepsilon_t$ conditionally independent of history given the current state, the law and the action). This is the standard assumption in state-space models — noise is a property of the observation channel at the moment of observation — stated on the typed target $S_t$ because on the state alone it is not well posed when the observation law is unknown (Discussion, "Why the law is in the conditioning"). The decomposition is a mathematical identity: a Pythagorean refinement of the bias-variance decomposition, with two orthogonality arguments in place of one. The positivity of $\mathbb{E}[\Vert\delta_t\Vert^2]$ follows from any of the three conditions; all typically hold simultaneously. The positivity of term (ii) specifically carries the alignment qualifier stated in the derivation — residual uncertainty about state or law must move the one-step conditional mean. The labels are relative to the declared $S_t$, like scope membership ( #scope-adaptive-system): a state component drawn afresh each step and seen through noise can be re-described as channel noise with no change to any observable, which moves its share from term (ii) to term (iii).

## Discussion

**Three floors, two kinds of "irreducible."** The excess term (i) is reducible by better modeling. The state-and-law floor is *irreducible by modeling* — it binds the Bayes-optimal predictor itself — but movable in two ways. By the law of total variance it splits into a part from state uncertainty at a given law, $\mathbb E[\operatorname{Var}(\bar o_t \mid \theta, \mathcal C_{t-1}, a_{t-1}) \mid \mathcal C_{t-1}, a_{t-1}]$, and a part from law uncertainty, $\operatorname{Var}(\mathbb E[\bar o_t \mid \theta, \mathcal C_{t-1}, a_{t-1}] \mid \mathcal C_{t-1}, a_{t-1})$. More informative action and observation choices shrink the state part, which is precisely the door through which active sensing and causal information yield ( #def-causal-information-yield) enter the mismatch budget. The law part shrinks as the history accumulates, even under passive observation, and for an invariant law that can be identified it decays toward zero along the run. The channel floor is irreducible outright — a property of the sensor, movable only by changing the instrument. The framework's calibration anchor exhibits the middle floor exactly: in #example-kalman, the steady-state *optimal* filter — well-specified by construction — has innovation variance $H P^- H^\top + R \gt R$; the excess $H P^- H^\top$ is the state-uncertainty floor, present at the in-class optimum.

**Reducible vs. irreducible (overfitting).** An agent that tries to eliminate *all* mismatch — including the two floors — will overfit: adjusting its model to explain noise, degrading future predictions. The update gain ( #emp-update-gain) implicitly separates signal from noise by weighting observations in proportion to their informativeness.

**What the persistence bounds contain.** Terms (i) and (ii) together are the expected square of the estimation error $\bar\delta_t = \bar o_t - \hat o_t$ ( #def-mismatch-signal): $\mathbb{E}\lVert\bar\delta_t\rVert^2 = \text{(i)} + \text{(ii)}$, the grouped "model error" above. That is the object whose dynamics the persistence results bound ( #deriv-sector-condition). Term (iii) is the part of the innovation that no correction can remove. A persistence ultimate bound therefore sits on (i)+(ii), and the innovation's mean square sits above it by exactly the channel floor.

**Connection to model sufficiency.** When $S(M_t) \lt 1$ ( #def-model-sufficiency), the model has lost predictive information relative to the full history. Under an alignment assumption (the lost information affects the one-step conditional mean), this implies a positive excess term (i). Without that alignment assumption, insufficiency still implies positive regret under proper scoring rules but not necessarily positive one-step mean error.

**Mismatch is structurally persistent.** In realistic AAT regimes, mismatch signals persist — they can be reduced but not eliminated when observation noise is non-degenerate. Deterministic, noiseless, perfectly specified systems are limiting edge cases, not the typical adaptive regime.

**Why the law is in the conditioning.** Conditioning on the state alone is enough when the observation law is known, and it stays enough when only the transition law is unknown: one-step prediction of $o_t$ needs only the current state, and uncertainty about the transition law reaches the decomposition only through term (ii). When the *observation* law is unknown and left out of the conditioning, fresh noise fails, and in general the identity fails with it. (The exception needs a symmetry that keeps the history from moving the posterior mean of the channel parameter.) Two descriptions of one process show this. Let $\theta \sim \mathcal N(0, \tau^2)$, $w_t \sim \mathcal N(0, q^2)$ and $\varepsilon_t \sim \mathcal N(0, r^2)$, all independent, with $w_t$ and $\varepsilon_t$ i.i.d. over $t$.

- In description A the parameter sits in the state: $\Omega_t = \theta + w_t$ and $o_t = \Omega_t + \varepsilon_t$.
- In description B it sits in the channel: $\Omega_t = w_t$ and $o_t = \Omega_t + \theta + \varepsilon_t$.

The observations have the same joint law, so the history, the Bayes predictor $\hat\theta_{t-1}$ and its posterior variance $\sigma_{t-1}^2 = \tau^2 v / (v + (t-1)\tau^2)$, with $v = q^2 + r^2$, are identical. For the Bayes agent $\mathbb E[\delta_t^2] = \sigma_{t-1}^2 + q^2 + r^2$. With state-only conditioning, A splits this exactly as $(q^2 + \sigma_{t-1}^2) + r^2$. B's state-only terms (ii) and (iii) are $q^2$ and $\tau^2 + r^2$. They sum to more than the truth by $\tau^2 - \sigma_{t-1}^2$, which is exactly the law information the history has extracted, booked as irreducible channel noise. Conditioning on $S_t = (\Omega_t, \theta)$ gives the exact split $(q^2 + \sigma_{t-1}^2) + r^2$ in both descriptions. So when the observation law is unknown, state-only fresh noise is not a property of the system: a bookkeeping choice that moves no observable fact makes it true or false. Housing the law in $S_t$ restores the identity and the meaning of its labels together. The other way out, leaving $\theta$ out of the conditioning but treating it as a fixed unknown constant, keeps the identity, but then term (i) contains a part no realizable agent can remove at finite $t$, and its label "reducible by modeling" is wrong. No treatment of an unhoused observation law keeps both.

#### Readers often ask / wonder

- "Why does $S(M_t) \lt 1$ not *automatically* imply positive squared-mismatch?" Because if the lost predictive information pertained only to the *variance* (not the *mean*) of the observation, $\Vert\delta_t\Vert^2$ wouldn't increase even though the model is objectively worse — squared mismatch is "blind" to certain information loss. This is exactly what the segment's alignment assumption names, and it may be the deeper motivation for the score-function mismatch $\tilde\delta$ which cares about the whole distribution (Claude, AUDIT-WORKING-773921). A reader reaching the alignment caveat will want this worked out.
