# Empirical discontinuity analysis

This toolkit tests the functional form in `#hyp-exponential-cognitive-load`: whether the time to make a change grows as $T = T_{\text{base}}(1+\alpha)^d$ in the number $d$ of boundary crossings in the changeset. The discontinuity score throughout is the toolkit's own, $d = (\text{file transitions}) + 2\cdot(\text{directory transitions})$, counted over a commit's changed paths in git's order.

## 1. The original fit measured the time estimator, not time

`analyze_git_history.py` assigns each commit a time `commit_time_estimate` $= 10 + 5n + 0.15L$ minutes, where $n$ is files changed and $L$ is lines changed. The saved dataset (`data/commits.json`, 229 commits of `~/src/_core/tst` from 2025-08-25 to 2025-08-29, one author) matches that formula to $10^{-12}$ minutes. The score $d$ is built from the same paths: over these commits $\operatorname{corr}(d, n) = 0.998$. Regressing the estimate on $d$ therefore fits the analyst's formula against a near-copy of one of its inputs.

`reproduce_estimate_fit.py` reproduces the original result exactly ($\alpha = 0.118$, $R^2 = 0.524$ at $d \le 25$, $N = 205$) and then changes the formula. Every variant returns an "exponential penalty" of about the same size, including formulas with no per-file term at all:

| Time formula (minutes) | $\alpha$ at $d \le 25$ | exponential $R^2$ | linear $R^2$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $10 + 5n + 0.15L$ (what the code ran) | 0.118 | 0.524 | 0.453 |
| $(30 + 15n + 0.5L)(1 + 0.2U)$, $U$ = directories | 0.131 | 0.617 | 0.532 |
| $10 + 0.15L$ (lines only) | 0.121 | 0.488 | 0.410 |
| $10 + 5n + 1.5L$ | 0.125 | 0.491 | 0.414 |
| $10 + 5n$ (files only) | 0.073 | 0.842 | 0.877 |

The $\alpha \approx 0.12$ is a property of how lines changed grow with files changed in that repository. No time and no comprehension enters it. When the estimate is exactly linear in $n$ (last row), an exponential fit still returns $\alpha = 0.07$ while fitting worse than a line: a single-form fit cannot test the form. The same dataset also does not support the claim that high-$d$ commits are fix-prone: commits with $d \gt 10$ carry a fix-like message 6.8% of the time against 11.4% for $d \le 10$.

## 2. Elapsed time between commits

`elapsed_time.py` replaces the estimate with a measured quantity: for each author, the time since that author's previous commit. This is elapsed wall-clock time, not instrumented working or comprehension time: it includes whatever else the author did, and how much work goes into one commit is the author's choice. It is, however, not computed from the counts that build $d$. The run used six histories (paths and HEADs in the script header): the original corpus, two repositories with direct commits by many authors (obsidian-linter, ink), and three where nearly every commit is a squash-merged pull request (anthropic-sdk-python, gemini-cli, codex). In squash-merged histories the timestamps record merge cadence rather than work, so those three are a check on the method, not a measurement, and are run with `--keep-squash`.

**Who the histories are.** The corpus is the repository in which TST itself was written (`~/src/_core/tst`): mostly Markdown, 149 of its 316 commits carrying a `Co-Authored-By: Claude` trailer, its 277 pairs at $d \le 25$ falling on twelve author-days. Its gaps are the cadence of agent-assisted document editing. In obsidian-linter and ink the pairs come mostly from one person: 71% of obsidian-linter's from one author email, and 80% of ink's from one of the three emails left once squash merges are out. That address is the maintainer's, who also commits under a second one, so the share from one person is higher.

**Squash merges inside direct-commit histories.** ink has 261 commits, and obsidian-linter 63, whose subjects end in a pull-request number "(#N)". All 63 of obsidian-linter's, and 191 of ink's, were committed by the web interface's merge; their timestamp is the merge time. (Equal author and commit dates do not distinguish them: most direct commits have them too.) A gap ending or starting at one measures merge cadence, so by default the script leaves out every pair with such a commit at either end. That removes 135 of ink's 493 pairs at $d \le 25$ and 81 of obsidian-linter's 1,264. Leaving out only pairs that end in one, or removing those commits before pairing, changes little: ink's working-time exponent is 0.26–0.30 under the three rules, obsidian-linter's 0.72–0.73. In ink, 70 of the 261 were committed locally rather than through the web interface; they too carry pull-request titles, and treating them as direct commits leaves ink's exponent at 0.30.

**Resampling.** Gaps within one working session are not independent, so every bootstrap resamples author-days (all pairs whose later commit falls on one day for one author). In the corpus this widens the intervals considerably: twelve author-days is a small sample.

**Long gaps.** A gap between two commits can hold hours or days away from the work, so how long gaps are treated decides what is measured. Dropping gaps above a session cap is not neutral: the share dropped grows with $d$. Over 24 hours it is 0.13, 0.20, 0.22, 0.25, 0.27 and 0.35 in obsidian-linter for $d$ = 0, 1–2, 3–5, 6–10, 11–25 and 26–60, and 0.18–0.34 in ink; in the corpus it is 0–0.06. Counting the long gaps as censored instead puts the time away into the median (obsidian-linter: 11, 46, 71, 130, 260 and 303 minutes). The script reports two treatments. Sections (1)–(5) keep gaps under caps from 60 minutes to none. Sections (6)–(8) keep every gap and fit a two-component log-normal mixture to $\log T$: a working component whose mean carries the form in $d$ (or in changeset size) and an idle component, with the working share free in each bin. The mixture is a modelling choice, and its numbers move with it.

**Results, $d \le 25$, direct-commit histories.**

- $d$ predicts elapsed time: under caps of 60–240 minutes, Spearman $\rho$ = 0.44–0.45 (corpus), 0.39–0.42 (obsidian-linter), 0.15–0.18 (ink), and partialling out lines changed leaves 0.36–0.37, 0.17–0.21 and 0.06–0.09. The mixture's working-time medians, in minutes for $d$ = 0, 1–2, 3–5, 6–10, 11–25: obsidian-linter 6.1, 12.1, 16.3, 33.6, 27.9 (last interval 16–50); corpus 3.1, 5.4, 5.6, 8.5, 16.4; ink 6.1, 7.1, 13.0, 11.8, 6.7, rising to the middle bin; whether it rises further the two top bins cannot say. The top bins' intervals are wide (ink's last is 1.3–137).
- The form, window-free (section 6). In the nested form $\log T = c + a\,d + b\log(1+d)$, the exponential predicts $a \gt 0$ with $b \approx 0$ and a power law the reverse. Inside the working component, obsidian-linter gives $b = 1.01$ $[0.61, 1.33]$ and $a = -0.08$ $[-0.18, +0.06]$: a pure exponential is rejected. Ink gives $b = 0.72$ $[-0.33, 1.29]$, the corpus $b = 0.32$ $[-0.30, 0.78]$ and $a = +0.04$ $[-0.01, +0.30]$: not distinguished. The working-time power exponent is 0.72 $[0.56, 0.88]$, 0.30 $[-0.04, 0.76]$ and 0.50 $[0.17, 0.84]$; the power law's log-likelihood exceeds the exponential's by 8.4, 0.8 and 1.6.
- Time away as two components (section 6, last line). This likelihood has several optima; the script takes the best of 30 starts and starts each bootstrap draw from it. In obsidian-linter the exponent becomes 0.86 $[0.69, 1.05]$, the power law still ahead of the exponential (by 4.3). In ink and the corpus the best fit is degenerate (ink: a narrow working spike, s.d. 0.17, beside an 8-minute "idle" component; corpus: an idle component with a median of 98 days), so the variant says nothing there. The one-idle fit starts with its idle component above the working one, which is the reading the method needs. Section (6) also refits each one-idle form from 30 random starts. In obsidian-linter and the corpus the reported fit is the best found. In ink every form's best fit swaps the labels (a 7-minute "idle" component, the time away carrying $d$) and beats the reported fit by 2.1 (exponential), 2.0 (power) and 1.4 (nested) in log-likelihood, so ink's numbers come from a local optimum chosen for its reading.
- The form, capped (sections 2 and 5). At caps of 60 minutes to 24 hours, $a$ is near zero or negative and $b$ is 0.38–0.39 (corpus), 0.74–1.04 (obsidian-linter) and 0.56–0.87 (ink). Without a cap, the corpus gives $a = +0.078$ $[-0.011, +0.173]$ and ink's coefficients are uninformative. The capped pattern does not decide by itself: a pure exponential in working time, mixed with idle gaps as fitted and passed through the same cap, also gives $b$ up to about 1 and sometimes a negative $a$. Over 1,000 replicates (section 7), that null reaches the observed $b$ in 0.015–0.095 of draws and the observed $a$ in 0.013–0.089, across histories and caps. The eight readings per history reuse the same gaps, so section (7) also sets each history's smallest tail share against the null replicates' own smallest. The null produces one as small in 0.047 (corpus), 0.058 (obsidian-linter) and 0.062 (ink) of its replicates, all within the simulation's error (about 0.007 at 1,000 replicates) of a 5% test. In obsidian-linter the fitted exponential puts working time at $d = 20$ (976 minutes) above its own idle median (509): it fits the long gaps at high $d$ by calling them work.
- The pure power slope of $\log T$ on $\log(1+d)$ is 0.24–0.76 under caps of 60 minutes to 24 hours, and reaches 0.89–0.90 in obsidian-linter with a one-week cap or none (section 5).
- A single exponential forced onto these data returns a per-discontinuity rate between 0.02 and 0.17 depending on the fitting method and the cap, and 0.07–0.30 inside the working component. That number describes the fit, not the data.

The squash-merged histories, run with `--keep-squash`, show the method's floor: $\rho$ = 0.07–0.12 for gemini-cli and codex. anthropic-sdk-python leaves too few same-author pairs ($N$ = 34–44 under caps of 60–240 minutes) for any conclusion.

**Changeset size.** The same instrument tests `#emp-changeset-size-principle`'s $t \propto \lvert\text{changeset}\rvert$, which predicts a log-log slope of 1. The slope of $\log T$ on $\log(1+L)$ is 0.14–0.26 (corpus), 0.24–0.50 (obsidian-linter) and 0.11–0.24 (ink) as the cap goes from 60 minutes to none, and 0.20 $[0.05, 0.49]$, 0.38 $[0.29, 0.67]$ and 0.17 $[-0.02, 0.36]$ inside the working component (section 8). On files touched the working-component slope is 0.68 $[0.29, 1.50]$, 0.90 $[0.69, 1.27]$ and 0.39 $[0.05, 0.75]$. Working-time medians by lines changed, obsidian-linter: 6.3, 16.1, 29.8, 31.0, 13.1 minutes for 0–10, 11–50, 51–200, 201–1000, over 1000 lines (median 4, 24, 100, 318, 2137 lines); about thirteen times the lines between the second and fourth bins brings 1.9 times the working time, or 2.5 times after subtracting the first bin's as overhead. In ink the fourth bin's median (8.4 minutes, 22 pairs) is below the second's (10.9), so that ratio is not defined.

## 3. What this does and does not bear on

At file and directory granularity, with elapsed time between commits as the time measure, the cost of a changeset rises with the number of boundary crossings and shows no compounding. In obsidian-linter a pure exponential is rejected for a power law with exponent 0.72, or 0.86 with time away modelled as two components; in ink and the corpus the forms cannot be told apart. What this measures is the count model: $d$ counts crossings without regard to whether understanding one change requires understanding another. The hypothesis's central open question, whether nested dependency among the scattered changes produces compounding that independent changes do not, is untested here. Testing it needs a measure of dependency among the changed locations, and a time measure closer to working time than inter-commit gaps. A test of either kind would supersede this one.

## Files and usage

- `analyze_git_history.py`: the original miner (`python analyze_git_history.py --repo <repo> --max-commits N --output data`). It writes `data/commits.json` and `data/analysis.pkl`, including the formula-based `commit_time_estimate` analyzed in §1.
- `analyze_outliers.py`: the original multi-form fit over filtering windows, which produced $\alpha = 0.118$; it reads `data/analysis.pkl`.
- `reproduce_estimate_fit.py`: §1. `python3 reproduce_estimate_fit.py data/commits.json`.
- `elapsed_time.py`: §2, on `git log` dumps made with the command in its header. `python3 elapsed_time.py [--keep-squash] <name>.log ...` (about a minute for the three direct-commit histories). Its sections (1)–(8) and its pairing rule are described in its docstring; every number in §2 is printed by one of them.
- `analyze_bug_detection.py`: a commit-message classifier for fix-like commits.
- `src/discontinuity_metrics.py`: a static per-file discontinuity counter. Its "predicted comprehension time" applies the hypothesis with an assumed $\alpha = 0.2$; that number is the hypothesis evaluated, not a measurement.
- `run_analysis.sh`: reproduces §1 from the saved dataset, which it never overwrites; given a repository path, it also mines that history into `rerun/`.

Dependencies: `requirements.txt` (numpy, scipy; matplotlib for `analyze_outliers.py`'s figure).
