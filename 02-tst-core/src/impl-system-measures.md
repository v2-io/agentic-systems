## Coupling, coherence, and the system-level measurement infrastructure

`#def-system-coupling` and `#def-system-coherence` give the chapter its aggregate measures: coupling as $P(\text{change } j \mid \text{change } i)$ between modules, coherence as expected proximity within modules. `#meas-coherence-coupling` makes both operational from git history. The implications-side payoff is what this measurement infrastructure *enables* beyond the segment-level definitions: it lifts Ch.3's structural-mechanics levers (alignment, size, proximity, discontinuity) from per-feature observables to *codebase-level* diagnostic quantities. Coupling between modules tells the practitioner where to invest in alignment (high-coupling-low-coherence module pairs are the highest-leverage refactor candidates); coherence tells the practitioner where modular structure is failing to match domain structure. The two measures are *git-computable*, making the diagnosis routinely available rather than requiring expert architectural review.

`#der-principled-decision-integration` is the chapter's synthesis result: optimal $C$ (a code-structure decision) minimizes $\mathbb E[T \mid C]$ across the feature-intensity profile $\lambda(F_i)$. The implication is concrete — *every code-structure decision is an investment under uncertainty about the future feature distribution*, and the framework supplies the principled basis for trading current implementation cost against future implementation cost via the dual-optimization formula. The connection back to `#der-change-expectation-baseline`'s Jeffreys-prior median predictor closes the loop: $\hat n_{\text{future}}$ supplies the prior on feature count; $\lambda(F_i)$ supplies the prior on feature-intensity distribution; principled-decision-integration combines them into the optimal $C$ that minimizes expected total cost. The framework's prescription is that *architectural decisions are forecasting problems* whose optimal posture depends on the prior, and the prior is empirically estimable from the system's own history.

The cross-domain transfer worth surfacing is OKR-as-observability-engineering, which the catalog draft surfaces as `#42`. The four classical OKR failure modes — vanity metrics (measuring what's easy rather than what matters), too many KRs (observability subgraph exceeds cognitive capacity), lagging indicators (measurement that lags causal effect), and Goodhart's Law (measurement becomes target, displacing underlying value) — map onto AAT predictions about observability subgraphs and credit-assignment-via-gradient (`#disc-credit-assignment-boundary`). The generalization is that *any multi-agent system with deep partially-observable strategy DAGs has the same four failure modes with the same formal forms*, regardless of domain. Mechanism design's literature (Holmström 1979 on "alignment of measurement and incentive"; Lazear 1989) has long surfaced these patterns under different names; the framework's contribution is that *one mechanism (observability subgraph + credit-assignment-via-gradient) produces all four*, converging four mechanism-design literatures into one structural account. The implication is that OKR-style measurement frameworks — and by extension, any system that observes proxies of underlying agent objectives — should be designed with the four failure modes as *predictable structural attractors*, not as engineering oversights.

## Operational availability and the continuous-operation extension

`#def-system-availability` and `#scope-continuous-operation` extend the optimization target to operational time: downtime is part of the feature-delivery cost from the user's perspective. The implications-side payoff is that the persistence machinery from AAT Part I Ch.4 extends to *operational persistence* — the codebase's ability to remain operational under stochastic failure rate $P(\text{fail})$ with recovery time $T_{\text{recovery}}$. The system's availability $\text{MTTF}/(\text{MTTF}+\text{MTTR})$ is the operational analog of the persistence-bandwidth-floor: high availability requires either high MTTF (rare failures — the structural-persistence side) or low MTTR (fast recovery — the bandwidth side). Both compose with the dual-optimization weighting: optimizing for low MTTF without low MTTR produces brittle systems where rare failures are catastrophic; optimizing for low MTTR without low MTTF produces resilient systems where failures are routine but cheap.

The framework's prescription for operational-system design is that *availability is a per-channel constraint that decomposes the same way as the developer-tempo channels from Ch.2's implications*. Different parts of a system have different failure-rate / recovery-time profiles, and aggregate availability metrics miss directional failures the practitioner cares about. The matrix-Loewner persistence condition from Part I Ch.4 implications applies here: the *weakest-component* availability is the bottleneck, and a system with 99.99% aggregate availability but 99% availability on a critical path is operationally fragile in a way the aggregate cannot detect. The cross-domain transfer to distributed-system reliability engineering is direct: the framework's prescription for SRE practice is that per-component availability instrumentation is structurally non-optional, not optimization polish.

## Limits of causal discovery from git history — and what they unlock

`#hyp-causal-discovery-from-git`'s `## Findings` covers the central result well. Each commit is a Pearl-Level-2 intervention $do(\text{change files})$; tests are reusable interventional probes with characterized $(\nu, U_o)$; three confounder classes (shared requirements, convention-driven bundling, developer knowledge state) weaken the interventional interpretation in the typical case; the unconfounded signal is a residual rather than the primary content. The framework's contribution is identifying *what kind of mathematical object git-derived data is* (Pearl-Level-2 interventional in character, with named confounders) rather than promising that causal-discovery algorithms will recover useful structure from it.

The implications-side payoff is the *operational distinction* between what git-derived analysis can and cannot tell us. Statistics that hold across the developer knowledge-state confounder — frequency asymmetries (changes to $A$ frequently followed by changes to $B$ but not vice versa), per-commit fingerprints that survive across multiple developers (suggesting the dependency lives in the code rather than in any one developer's mental model), test-failure patterns conditional on commit content — are *interventionally* readable. Statistics that confound with developer knowledge state — co-change frequency without temporal asymmetry, individual-developer-specific patterns, conventions-driven-bundling correlations — are *associational* only. The framework's prescription for mining-software-repositories research is that *the same data supports interventional and associational queries differently*, and the prior-art literature has often confused the two. The framework supplies the Pearl-causal-hierarchy vocabulary that distinguishes them.

The composition with `#obs-software-epistemic-properties` (Ch.1) closes a load-bearing arc: P5 (exact recording of $\mathcal C_t^{\text{commit}}$) is what makes git-derived data different from typical observational corpora — the chronicle is cryptographically immutable, signed-author, mainline-bounded, and universally retrievable. The framework's 14 EXACT estimators for AAT/TST quantities all rely on this property. The implication is that *agentic systems developing software accumulate a Pearl-Level-2 chronicle by default*, and the framework's reach into the developer-agent setting is via this accumulated chronicle. The framework predicts that AI-coding-agent productivity improvements will increasingly come from *better mining of accumulated git history* (treating git as the agent's interventional training corpus) rather than from better model training on observational corpora — and the prediction has an empirical signature (agent productivity should correlate with the depth and quality of the codebase's commit history in ways that observational training cannot explain).

## The calibration-laboratory transfer theorem — methodological closing

The chapter is where TST closes, and the framework's methodological position deserves naming at the chapter-end. `#41` in the catalog draft surfaces a candidate-derivable *transfer theorem*: any AAT finding's transfer from software to a non-software domain inherits an identifiability-floor instance for each $P_i$ (from `#obs-software-epistemic-properties`) the target domain fails. The framework should therefore have a transfer theorem of the form "domain $D$ inherits AAT result $X$ under the identifiability-floor escapes corresponding to $\{P_i : D \text{ fails } P_i\}$." Without this, the calibration-lab framing is honest about scope but offers no machinery for what survives transfer.

The implications-side reading is methodological: the candidate transfer theorem is the framework's *closing methodological move* for TST. It compose `#obs-software-epistemic-properties`'s six properties from Ch.1 with `#disc-identifiability-floor`'s pattern from AAT's meta-architecture, and supplies the discipline for any AAT-machinery transfer from software to non-software domains. The framework's posture is that domain-transfer is not "AAT machinery applies anywhere" — it is "AAT machinery applies wherever the identifiability assumptions hold; where they don't, the framework predicts exactly which escapes are required." This converts the calibration-laboratory framing from a methodological aspiration into operational machinery for any practitioner extending AAT results beyond software.

The cross-domain examples the framework would prescribe are concrete. Transferring AAT's persistence-bandwidth-floor result from software to biology: the target domain fails P5 (no cryptographically immutable chronicle), so the bandwidth-floor result transfers with an identifiability-floor escape via sampled-rather-than-exhaustive observation; the operational consequence is that biological persistence-bandwidth analyses must be calibrated against sampled chronica with explicit gap accounting. Transferring AAT's causal-discovery machinery from software to organizational behavior: the target fails P2 (no executable counterfactuals) and P3 (limited genuine interventions), so the machinery transfers under the identifiability-floor Instance 1 escape (loop-Level-2 access via observable-sibling-covariance under organizational experimentation, when ethics permit). Each transfer is principled rather than assumed, and the framework's discipline is the transfer-assumption table that makes each escape explicit.
